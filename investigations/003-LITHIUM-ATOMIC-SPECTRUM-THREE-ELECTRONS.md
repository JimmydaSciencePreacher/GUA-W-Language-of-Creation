# Investigation 003
# Lithium's Atomic Spectrum: Three Electrons, Shell Structure, and the Limits of the Helium Comparison

**Disciplines:** Atomic physics, quantum mechanics, spectroscopy, physical chemistry  
**Status:** Initial investigation with Four-Question Study Method applied  
**Primary subject:** Neutral lithium (Li I), with lithium ions distinguished where relevant

## 1. Starting question

Hydrogen has one electron. Neutral helium has two interacting electrons. Neutral lithium has three electrons. What changes when the third electron is present, and how do the inner two electrons and the outer electron participate in the atom's organization and observable spectrum?

The central question is not simply how a third particle changes the count. Lithium provides a case in which two electrons occupy the inner 1s shell while a third occupies the 2s state in the ground-state configuration. This makes it possible to investigate the relationship between an inner electron pair, an outer electron, nuclear attraction, electron–electron repulsion, shielding, and measured spectral lines.

The comparison must distinguish neutral lithium (Li I), singly ionized lithium (Li II), and more highly ionized lithium. Their electron counts and structures differ.

## 2. Scientific terminology

- **Neutral lithium (Li I):** An atom with three protons in its nucleus and three electrons, with net electric charge zero.
- **Lithium-7 and lithium-6:** The two stable lithium isotopes. They have the same number of protons and electrons when neutral, but different numbers of neutrons and different nuclear masses and spins.
- **Singly ionized lithium (Li II):** Lithium with one electron removed, leaving two electrons. Its ground state is 1s².
- **Doubly ionized lithium (Li III):** Lithium with two electrons removed, leaving one electron. It is hydrogen-like.
- **Electron configuration:** A notation describing the distribution of electrons among atomic orbitals. Neutral lithium's ground-state configuration is 1s²2s.
- **Core electrons:** The two 1s electrons in ground-state neutral lithium.
- **Valence electron:** The 2s electron in ground-state neutral lithium; it is the outer electron involved in many chemical interactions and prominent optical transitions.
- **Shielding:** The reduction in the net nuclear attraction experienced by an electron because other electrons contribute to the electric field and the atom's quantum distribution.
- **Effective nuclear charge:** A useful description of the net nuclear attraction experienced by an electron after accounting for shielding. It is not a literal change in the proton count.
- **Electron correlation:** The coordinated behavior of electrons arising from their mutual interaction and the quantum state, beyond a simple independent-electron approximation.
- **Fine structure:** Small energy separations associated with relativistic and spin–orbit effects, among other corrections.
- **Resonance line:** A transition between an excited state and the ground state, or vice versa through absorption, that is particularly prominent in a spectrum.
- **Doublet:** A pair of closely spaced spectral lines associated here with transitions involving the two fine-structure levels of the 2p state.
- **Ionization energy:** The energy required to remove an electron from an atom or ion in a specified state.

## 3. Scientific structure: three electrons in neutral lithium

The neutral lithium ground-state configuration is 1s²2s. The two 1s electrons form the inner electron pair, and the third electron occupies the 2s orbital.

The electrons are not arranged as three small particles travelling along fixed classical tracks. Orbitals are quantum-mechanical descriptions of the states and probability distributions used to characterize the electrons.

In a simplified nonrelativistic fixed-nucleus model, the electronic Hamiltonian contains kinetic-energy terms, attraction between each electron and the nucleus, and repulsion between every pair of electrons:

\[
H =
-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\nabla_i^2
-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{3e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_i}
+\sum_{i<j}\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_{ij}}
\]

Here, r_i is the distance of electron i from the nucleus and r_ij is the separation between electrons i and j. The nuclear charge is +3e. The final sum contains three pairwise electron–electron repulsion terms.

This model makes a key difference visible. Hydrogen has no electron–electron repulsion term. Neutral helium has one such pair interaction. Neutral lithium has three pairwise interactions. In an accurate treatment, the electron motions are coupled through these interactions, and the total wavefunction must satisfy the exchange symmetry required for identical electrons.

The displayed Hamiltonian is a simplified starting point. Precision calculations must also account for finite nuclear mass and, as needed, relativistic and quantum-electrodynamic corrections.

## 4. Core and valence organization

The 1s²2s configuration is often described using a core-plus-valence picture:

- The two 1s electrons form a compact inner core.
- The 2s electron occupies an outer orbital.
- The inner electrons contribute to the electric field experienced by the outer electron.
- The outer electron's energy and spatial distribution are affected by both nuclear attraction and the other electrons.

This description is useful, but it is an approximation to the full interacting quantum system. The electrons are indistinguishable; they are not permanently assigned individual identities as "core electron one," "core electron two," and "outer electron." The configuration notation identifies an overall state and its dominant orbital occupation.

The concept of shielding helps explain why the outer electron does not experience the full +3e nuclear attraction as though the inner electrons were absent. The net attraction depends on the electron distribution and cannot be reduced to a fixed integer subtraction at every distance. Effective nuclear charge is a useful model concept, not an additional physical particle or a literal alteration of the nucleus.

## 5. What is observed?

NIST's evaluated data list the neutral lithium ground state as 1s²2s, with term designation ²S₁/₂. The first excited configuration includes 1s²2p, with two fine-structure levels:

- 1s²2p ²P°₁/₂ at 14903.622 cm⁻¹;
- 1s²2p ²P°₃/₂ at 14903.957 cm⁻¹.

The two levels give rise to the prominent lithium resonance doublet near 670.8 nm. NIST's persistent-line table lists components at approximately 6707.775 Å and 6707.926 Å, corresponding to about 670.7775 nm and 670.7926 nm.

The two lines are close together, but they are not identical. Their separation is associated with the fine-structure splitting of the 2p level. Whether an instrument resolves them separately depends on its resolving power and on the conditions that broaden the lines.

Other neutral-lithium transitions occur in the ultraviolet, visible, and infrared regions. NIST lists, for example, lines near 610.35 nm, 460.28 nm, and 812.6 nm, each connected to specified upper and lower energy levels.

The spectrum therefore provides more than a recognizable color. The line positions and their fine components provide quantitative evidence about the energy structure of the atom.

## 6. Scientific explanation of lithium's spectrum

The prominent 670.8 nm doublet is associated with transitions between the 1s²2s ²S₁/₂ ground level and the two 1s²2p ²P° fine-structure levels.

The 2p state is split into levels with total electronic angular momentum J = 1/2 and J = 3/2. The small energy difference between these levels produces two nearby transition wavelengths.

The energy difference between a pair of levels determines the photon frequency:

\[
\Delta E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda_{vac}}
\]

The observed intensity of a line is not determined by the energy difference alone. It also depends on the population of the upper state, the transition probability, the physical conditions of the source, optical depth, and the instrument's response.

Lithium's outer 2s electron is relatively weakly bound compared with the inner 1s electrons. NIST lists the first ionization energy of neutral lithium as approximately 5.3917 eV. This is the energy needed to remove the outer electron from neutral lithium in its ground state, leaving Li II with a 1s² ground configuration.

The ionization energy and spectral lines are related aspects of the same energy-level structure, but they are not interchangeable measurements.

## 7. Distinguishing neutral lithium from its ions

The label Li I means neutral lithium, not lithium with one electron. The spectroscopic numeral I denotes the neutral atom. Li II denotes singly ionized lithium, which has two electrons. Li III denotes doubly ionized lithium, which has one electron.

This distinction matters for the comparison:

- **Li I:** Three electrons; ground configuration 1s²2s.
- **Li II:** Two electrons; ground configuration 1s². This is a helium-like ion, but with nuclear charge +3e rather than +2e.
- **Li III:** One electron; hydrogen-like ion with nuclear charge +3e.

A lithium-ion spectrum cannot be treated as if it were the spectrum of neutral lithium. The electron count and nuclear charge together determine the atomic or ionic system being described.

## 8. GUA-W descriptive examination

The following mapping describes relationships established by the scientific account. It does not replace the quantum-mechanical explanation.

### Structure
Neutral lithium is an interacting three-electron system with a nucleus of charge +3e. Its ground-state configuration is 1s²2s. The core-plus-valence description identifies a useful organization within the state, while the complete quantum state remains the more accurate description of the interacting electrons.

### Framework
The physical framework includes nuclear attraction, electron–electron repulsion, and the quantum properties of electrons. The mathematical framework includes the many-electron Schrödinger description and methods used to approximate or calculate atomic states. These physical and mathematical meanings must not be collapsed.

### Pattern
The measured spectrum contains discrete lines, including the prominent resonance doublet near 670.8 nm. The doublet and other transitions form a measurable pattern associated with differences between atomic energy levels.

### Constraint
Specific constraints and conditions include:
- the allowed quantum states of the three-electron system;
- exchange symmetry for identical electrons;
- angular momentum, parity, and transition selection rules;
- conservation of energy and angular momentum;
- the atom's ionization and excitation conditions;
- line broadening, optical depth, and instrumental resolution in the measurement.

These are distinct physical, quantum, environmental, and measurement constraints. They should not be merged into one vague category.

### Purpose
The lithium spectrum does not establish an ultimate or intentional purpose for the atom. Spectroscopy has a human scientific function: it can identify lithium and measure atomic properties. That use is not evidence of the atom's ultimate purpose. No ultimate-purpose claim is assigned here.

### Manifestation
The observable manifestation is electromagnetic radiation at particular wavelengths and intensities under specified conditions. The resonance doublet is one measurable feature of lithium's energy structure.

### Witness
The witness chain includes the lithium sample or source, emitted or absorbed photons, optical system, spectrometer, detector, calibration standards, recorded spectrum, data analysis, and evaluated reference data. The measurement is mediated and has limits; a spectrum is a registered result, not a complete image of the atom.

## 9. Relationship audit: hydrogen, helium, and lithium

### What remains comparable?

All three neutral atoms have quantum states and discrete energy levels. Radiative transitions are associated with photons whose energies correspond to differences between atomic levels. Spectroscopy provides a measurable witness to those transitions.

### What changes?

- **Hydrogen:** One electron; no electron–electron repulsion.
- **Helium:** Two interacting electrons; one pairwise electron–electron interaction; singlet and triplet state systems.
- **Lithium:** Three interacting electrons; three pairwise electron–electron interactions; a 1s² core-plus-valence ground-state configuration and a prominent resonance doublet.

The growing electron count changes the many-electron problem. It does not mean that the atoms are merely repetitions of one another with one more particle added.

### Classification

- **Same broad physical principles:** Quantum states, electromagnetic transitions, conservation laws, and spectroscopic measurement apply to all three.
- **Different detailed interacting systems:** The number of electrons and pairwise interactions differs; helium and lithium require different many-electron state descriptions.
- **Comparable GUA-W relationship:** Organized atomic states, physical interactions, transition constraints, observable radiation, and measurement-based witness can be described in each case.
- **Not established:** That the shared descriptive vocabulary constitutes a new physical mechanism or that all systems share one detailed structure.

## 10. Boundary and participation question

Lithium offers a useful examination of distinctions, but the investigation must not turn every distinction into a physical boundary.

- **Core and valence description:** The division between 1s core and 2s valence electron is a useful classification of orbital occupation. It is not a hard spatial wall inside the atom.
- **Shielding:** The inner-electron distribution changes the effective attraction experienced by the outer electron. This is an interaction and field relationship, not a material membrane.
- **Ionization:** Removing the outer electron changes the charge state and electron count. The ionization threshold is an energy condition, not a physical surface the electron must cross.
- **Spectrometer resolution:** The instrument may or may not resolve the close doublet components. That is a measurement limit, not a boundary in the atom.

**Finding:** The core–valence distinction helps organize the description, but it is not a material interface. The boundary-participation question remains open for a system with a demonstrable physical interface or transport boundary.

## 12. Four-Question Study Method

### Question 1 — What must AI ask to understand GUA-W?

The investigation must ask whether the third electron changes the organization of the system in a way that cannot be captured by merely adding one component to the helium description.

Questions exposed:
- What exactly is meant by *Structure*: electron count, orbital configuration, full quantum state, or core-plus-valence description?

- Does shielding describe a real interaction effect, a model summary, or both?
- Which parts of the spectrum arise from energy-level differences, and which fine details require additional physical corrections?
- Are the lithium lines being attributed to neutral Li I or to an ion? The spectroscopic designation must remain explicit.
- Does the GUA-W vocabulary clarify the relationships without substituting for the many-electron physics?

**Finding:** The vocabulary must identify the level of description. The atom, electron configuration, quantum state, effective model, spectral transition, and detector record are related but distinct.

### Question 3 — What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

Lithium demonstrates that the third electron introduces a useful core-plus-valence description. The inner 1s² pair and outer 2s electron occupy different roles in the ground-state configuration. The outer electron experiences nuclear attraction modified by the presence and distribution of the inner electrons.

The resonance doublet near 670.8 nm provides a measurable witness to the fine-structure separation of the 2p levels. The spectrum also demonstrates why ion identity matters: Li I, Li II, and Li III are different systems with different electron counts.

**Finding:** The additional electron changes the interacting system and its energy structure. The observed doublet is a manifestation of fine-structure splitting, not merely a color label or a direct picture of electron arrangement.

### Question 4 — What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

The spectrum is mediated by a measurement chain. The atom interacts with radiation; the spectrometer separates wavelengths; the detector records intensity; calibration and analysis establish line positions; and atomic theory interprets the levels and transitions.

The investigator must distinguish the measured line from the inferred state structure and must state the ionization stage. AI is also a derivative witness here: it examines published atomic data and scientific explanations rather than directly performing the experiment.

**Finding:** Reliable witnessing requires identifying the system, the measurement conditions, the instrument's limits, and the inferential steps between recorded signal and atomic interpretation.

## 13. Recurring comparison questions

### Same mechanism, similar relationship, or similar appearance?

Hydrogen, helium, and lithium share the broad quantum principles of atomic states and radiative transitions. Their detailed electron interactions and state structures differ.

**Classification:** Same broad physical principles; different detailed atomic mechanisms and state structures; comparable GUA-W descriptive relationships. This does not establish one identical detailed mechanism across all three atoms.

### Does a boundary merely separate two regions, or can it also participate in the relationship between them?

The core–valence description is a classification within the atom, not a hard material boundary. Shielding is an interaction effect, not evidence of a membrane. The spectrometer's resolution is a measurement constraint, not an atomic boundary.

**Classification:** Useful internal distinction and interaction identified; no material boundary participation established in this investigation. The broader question remains open.

## 14. Direction emerging for the next investigation

The three-atom comparison now gives us a clearer opportunity to ask a different kind of question. Rather than immediately adding another electron and moving to beryllium, a next investigation could examine how atomic spectra are used to identify matter in an environment where the source cannot be handled directly.

A promising direction is **spectral identification in stellar light**:

> How do astronomers infer the presence and physical conditions of elements such as hydrogen, helium, and lithium from light received from stars, and how do temperature, ionization, line formation, and radiative transfer affect what is actually witnessed?

This would move from isolated atomic structure to the relationship between matter, radiation, an extended source, and a distant observer. It would also give the boundary question a more direct setting through stellar-atmosphere layers, optical depth, and the formation of spectral lines.

This is a proposed direction arising from the current investigations, not a fixed order for the sciences. The next study should first narrow the question and identify sources that distinguish atomic transition data from stellar-atmosphere interpretation.
