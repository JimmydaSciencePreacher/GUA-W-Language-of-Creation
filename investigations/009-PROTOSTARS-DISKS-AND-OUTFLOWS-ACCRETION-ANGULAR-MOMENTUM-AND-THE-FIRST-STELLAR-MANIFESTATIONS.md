# Protostars, Disks, and Outflows: Accretion, Angular Momentum, and the First Stellar Manifestations

**Disciplines:** Star-formation astrophysics, molecular astrophysics, radio and infrared astronomy, spectroscopy, magnetohydrodynamics, plasma physics, radiative transfer

## 1. Starting question

Investigation 008 examined filaments and dense cores, including the evidence used to distinguish a dense concentration from a gravitationally bound prestellar core. The next inquiry follows the physical evidence into the earliest observable stages of stellar formation.

**How does collapsing core material become a protostar and circumstellar disk, how is angular momentum redistributed, and what do jets and molecular outflows reveal about accretion and the surrounding environment?**

This question does not presume that every core follows one identical pathway, that every observed disk is in equilibrium, or that every outflow has the same launching mechanism. Low-mass and high-mass protostars share important features but differ in their environments, radiation fields, accretion rates, and feedback.

## 2. The physical system: envelope, protostar, disk, jet, and outflow

A young embedded stellar system may include several connected components:

- **Infalling envelope:** The surrounding gas and dust reservoir from which material can move toward the central system.
- **Protostar:** A forming stellar object, not yet in the same state as a mature hydrogen-burning main-sequence star.
- **Circumstellar disk:** Rotating gas and dust orbiting the central protostar. Material can move through the disk toward the central object.
- **Jet:** A narrow, fast, often highly collimated flow emerging from the inner star–disk region.
- **Disk wind:** A broader flow launched from disk regions; it can carry mass, energy, and angular momentum away.
- **Molecular outflow:** Often slower, wider-angle molecular gas accelerated or entrained by the primary jet and/or wind as it interacts with the surrounding envelope.
- **Outflow cavity:** A region cleared, heated, or reshaped by the flow through the envelope.

These components are related but not interchangeable. A jet is not simply another name for the entire molecular outflow. A disk is not the envelope. The central protostar is not the whole protostellar system.

The physical system changes with time. Observations commonly classify young stellar objects by their spectral energy distributions and envelope properties, but class labels are observational schemes, not perfectly synchronized evolutionary clocks. Inclination, extinction, multiplicity, and environment can affect the classification.

## 3. From prestellar core to protostar

A gravitationally unstable core can contract. As the density rises, gravitational potential energy is converted into internal energy and motion. Radiation, gas dynamics, chemistry, and optical depth affect the thermal evolution.

For low-mass star formation, theoretical descriptions commonly include an early collapse phase, formation of a first hydrostatic core in suitable models, and subsequent formation of a central protostar after further contraction. The details depend on thermodynamics, magnetic fields, rotation, and the treatment of non-ideal effects.

The stages are reconstructed from observations of different objects and from numerical models. Astronomers do not generally watch one individual core continuously from its initial state through the complete formation of a star. Evolutionary sequences are inferred by comparing populations, physical diagnostics, and models.

### What observations contribute

- Dust continuum emission traces the distribution of cool material, subject to temperature and opacity assumptions.
- Molecular lines reveal gas kinematics, excitation, and chemical conditions.
- Infrared emission can reveal warm dust and embedded sources, though extinction and optical depth may hide the central object.
- Radio and millimetre observations can detect compact structures and molecular flows.
- Variability and luminosity changes can provide evidence of changing accretion activity.

A detected compact source is not automatically a fully characterized protostar. The identification depends on wavelength, resolution, source association, and supporting evidence.

## 4. Angular momentum: why collapse does not simply fall straight inward

A molecular core generally contains some angular momentum. In the absence of effective angular-momentum transport, infalling material cannot all move directly to the central object while retaining its original angular momentum. Rotation becomes increasingly important as material contracts.

For a particle in a simplified axisymmetric gravitational potential, the specific angular momentum is:

\[
j = r v_{\phi}
\]

where \(r\) is cylindrical radius and \(v_{\phi}\) is azimuthal velocity.

For a circular Keplerian orbit around a central mass \(M_*\):

\[
v_{\phi}=\sqrt{\frac{G M_*}{r}}
\]

and therefore:

\[
j=\sqrt{G M_* r}
\]

These expressions are idealized. A real protostellar system includes gas pressure, self-gravity, magnetic stresses, turbulence, non-axisymmetric structure, and material inflow. Nevertheless, they illustrate why angular momentum is central to disk formation and transport.

### Angular-momentum transport

Material can move inward through a disk only if angular momentum is redistributed. Possible contributions include:
- magnetic stresses;
- magnetohydrodynamic disk winds;
- gravitational torques from disk structure or companions;
- hydrodynamic turbulence and instabilities;
- spiral waves and non-axisymmetric structures;
- interactions with infalling envelope material.

The relative importance of these processes varies. A statement that “friction makes the disk accrete” is too vague unless the physical transport mechanism is specified.

## 5. Disk formation and the magnetic-braking problem

Conservation of angular momentum makes disk formation a natural expectation during rotating collapse. However, magnetic fields can couple the collapsing gas to its surroundings and transfer angular momentum outward. This process is called **magnetic braking**.

Early idealized calculations with strongly coupled magnetic fields sometimes found it difficult to form large rotationally supported disks under realistic field strengths. This became known as the magnetic-braking problem or, in some literature, the “magnetic braking catastrophe.”

Research has examined ways in which disk formation can occur under more realistic conditions:
- non-ideal magnetohydrodynamic effects, including Ohmic dissipation, ambipolar diffusion, and the Hall effect;
- misalignment between rotation and magnetic-field directions;
- turbulence and asymmetric density structure;
- changing ionization and dust properties;
- complex initial conditions and environmental accretion.

These are active areas of investigation, not a single settled explanation that applies identically to every protostar.

### Keplerian rotation

A disk is often described as Keplerian when its rotational velocity is approximately consistent with orbital motion dominated by a central mass:

\[
v_{\phi}(r) \approx \sqrt{\frac{G M_*}{r}}
\]

Observed rotation curves can support the presence of a rotationally supported disk. But line emission traces particular molecules and excitation conditions, and projection, optical depth, infall, outflow contamination, and limited resolution can complicate interpretation.

A rotating flattened structure is not automatically a Keplerian disk. The dynamical evidence matters.

## 6. What a disk does—and what it does not establish

A circumstellar disk is a region where gas and dust orbit the young central object. It can transport mass inward, redistribute angular momentum, heat and cool, develop substructure, and evolve chemically and dynamically.

The disk's observed appearance depends on:
- wavelength and optical depth;
- dust temperature and grain properties;
- gas and dust distribution;
- inclination;
- spatial resolution;
- beam convolution and interferometric filtering;
- chemical abundance and excitation;
- the central source's luminosity and variability.

Rings, gaps, spirals, asymmetries, and compact emission peaks may have several possible explanations. They can arise from planets, companions, pressure maxima, snowlines, dust evolution, gravitational instability, magnetic effects, or observational limitations. A ring is not, by itself, proof of a planet.

The early embedded disk and a later protoplanetary disk are related phases but should not be treated as identical physical environments.

## 7. Accretion: material moving toward the protostar

Accretion is the transfer of material toward and onto the central protostar. It can occur through multiple connected scales:
1. cloud material enters a filament or core;
2. envelope material moves toward the central system;
3. streamers or other non-axisymmetric flows can feed the disk;
4. disk processes transport material inward;
5. material crosses the inner disk region and is accreted by the protostar.

The flow need not be smooth or steady. Accretion can be episodic, variable, and affected by companions, disk instabilities, magnetic processes, and changes in the supply from the envelope.

Accretion luminosity is often discussed through the approximate relation:

\[
L_{acc} \sim \frac{G M_* \dot{M}}{R_*}
\]

where \(M_*\) is protostellar mass, \(\dot{M}\) is the accretion rate, and \(R_*\) is protostellar radius. The expression is an order-of-magnitude estimate; the fraction of gravitational energy radiated depends on the flow geometry and where energy is released.

Luminosity bursts and variability can provide evidence of changing accretion, but the inferred accretion history depends on extinction, bolometric corrections, source geometry, and time sampling.

## 8. Jets, disk winds, and molecular outflows

Young stellar objects often exhibit bipolar outflows. Observations distinguish several related structures:

### Collimated jets

Jets are narrow, fast flows, often traced by shock-excited atomic, ionic, or molecular emission. Their knots and bow shocks can record episodic ejection and interaction with surrounding material.

### Disk winds

Disk winds are broader flows launched from disk regions in magnetized models. They may carry angular momentum away from the disk and help enable accretion. The launch region, field geometry, and relative contribution of different wind components remain subjects of research.

### Molecular outflows

Molecular outflows often trace ambient gas accelerated by the jet or wind. They can be slower and broader than the primary jet. Their observed momentum and energy provide clues to the interaction between the young system and its envelope.

These categories describe distinct parts of a coupled flow. Observations may not always cleanly separate the jet, wind, and entrained molecular component.

## 9. Why accretion and ejection are linked

A central physical problem is how disk material loses angular momentum and moves inward. Outflows provide a possible and, in some systems, strongly supported channel for carrying angular momentum away.

Magnetohydrodynamic models describe gas coupled to magnetic fields and moving along or across field structures. Rotating disk winds can transport angular momentum from the disk. Collimated jets may emerge from inner regions, while wider winds can be launched from a range of disk radii.

The broad relationship is:

- material is supplied to the disk;
- angular momentum must be redistributed for some material to move inward;
- some mass and angular momentum can be carried outward in winds or jets;
- the flow interacts with the envelope and surrounding cloud.

This is not a claim that every accretion event has one unique outflow response, or that outflows alone account for all angular-momentum transport.

A particularly important distinction: **the material accreted by the protostar and the material ejected into the outflow are not necessarily the same parcels of gas.** The system redistributes mass, energy, and angular momentum across different regions.

## 10. Observational evidence: what instruments actually witness

Different tracers reveal different components:

- **CO rotational lines:** Often trace molecular gas in extended outflows and envelope motions.
- **SiO:** Can trace shocked gas where silicon-bearing material is released or produced in shocks.
- **H₂ emission:** Can trace warm shocked molecular gas in jets and outflow structures.
- **[Fe II] and other atomic/ionic lines:** Can trace energetic, partially ionized jet components.
- **SO and other sulfur-bearing molecules:** Can trace shocked or chemically distinctive regions, depending on environment.
- **Dust continuum:** Traces dust emission from disks, envelopes, and compact structures.
- **Molecular rotation signatures:** Can constrain disk kinematics and, in favorable cases, rotation in outflows.
- **Polarization:** Provides projected magnetic-field orientation information, subject to grain alignment and line-of-sight integration.
- **Infrared spectroscopy:** Can reveal warm gas, dust, and emission lines near embedded sources.

No tracer is a universal map of all gas. Each line has excitation requirements, optical-depth behavior, chemical dependencies, and instrumental limitations.

### Direct observation versus physical reconstruction

A telescope records radiation. A calibrated image or spectrum is a processed measurement. A velocity field, disk mass, accretion rate, or wind-launching radius is inferred using additional assumptions and models.

A clear report distinguishes:
1. detector and instrument data;
2. calibrated images or spectra;
3. measured intensity, position, and line-of-sight velocity;
4. derived physical quantities;
5. model interpretation of the mechanism.

This distinction is essential when using words such as “direct evidence.”

## 11. Case study: accretion streamers and jets in VLA 1623–2417

The 2024 FAUST study of the VLA 1623–2417 protostellar cluster used ALMA observations at approximately 50 au resolution. The authors reported extended sulfur monoxide emission and identified a long, accelerating streamer plausibly feeding the VLA 1623 B protostar.

The study is important because it links structures on envelope scales with material plausibly reaching a protostellar system. However, “plausibly feeding” is appropriately qualified: the observed emission and kinematics support an interpretation, but the complete three-dimensional flow and every parcel's history are not directly observed.

The case illustrates why a protostellar system cannot always be treated as an isolated disk. Material can arrive through asymmetric streamers and interact with multiple sources in a cluster.

## 12. Case study: JWST and ALMA observations of a Class I protobinary

A 2024 JWST JOYS study of the TMC1-E/W Class I protobinary system reported different outflow signatures from the two components. It identified an H₂ outflow and extended ionic emission associated with TMC1-E, an accretion streamer from larger than 1,000 au scales, and a collimated jet associated with TMC1-W.

The authors interpreted the observations as evidence that accretion and ejection are intertwined at different scales. The study is especially useful because it combines infrared spectroscopy with ALMA observations and resolves multiple processes within a binary system.

The two protostars do not exhibit identical observed flows. A binary system can contain different accretion and ejection conditions even when the components share a broader environment.

## 13. Feedback: how outflows alter their surroundings

Jets and winds can:
- excavate cavities in the envelope;
- shock and heat surrounding gas;
- inject momentum and energy into the cloud;
- alter the material available for future accretion;
- influence nearby dense structures;
- contribute to the regulation of star formation.

Outflow feedback can both disturb and redistribute the environment. Whether it compresses nearby gas enough to contribute to subsequent collapse, or disperses material and suppresses further accretion, depends on geometry, energy, momentum, density, cooling, and the local environment.

A cavity is evidence of interaction, but does not alone establish whether the net effect on a neighboring core is triggering or suppression.

Feedback must be evaluated at the scale of the process: the disk, envelope, core, cloud, or larger star-forming region. A result at one scale cannot automatically be generalized to all others.

## 14. Boundary and participation question

The protostellar system provides several examples where interfaces may participate in exchange:

- **Disk–envelope interface:** Infalling material may cross into the disk or be redirected by the disk's gravitational and magnetic environment.
- **Disk–wind launching region:** Magnetic stresses and gas motion can transfer angular momentum and energy into an outflow.
- **Jet–envelope interface:** Shocks transfer momentum and energy to surrounding material.
- **Outflow cavity wall:** Entrained gas and shocked material can reshape the envelope.
- **Protostar–inner disk interface:** Material crosses the inner accretion region and contributes to protostellar growth.

These are not all equivalent boundaries. Some are physical interaction regions; others are approximate surfaces used in models or observations. Their locations can change over time and may not be sharply defined.

A drawn disk edge or an intensity contour is not automatically a material wall. A physical interface is established by the relevant dynamics, transport, and evidence.

**Finding:** In this system, boundaries can participate in transfer and interaction, but the role of each interface must be demonstrated separately. Do not infer that every boundary is active or that every exchange uses the same mechanism.

## 15. What is established, supported, and unresolved?

### Well-established observational picture

- Embedded protostellar systems can contain a central young stellar object, surrounding disk, infalling envelope, and outflow structures.
- Rotationally supported disks are observed around young stellar objects.
- Jets and molecular outflows are common in many phases of star formation.
- Accretion and ejection occur together in observed young systems.
- Outflows transfer momentum and energy to surrounding material.
- Magnetic fields and magnetohydrodynamic processes are important in current models of disk evolution and outflow launching.

### Supported physical account

Angular momentum is a central constraint on collapse and disk accretion. Magnetic stresses, gravitational torques, hydrodynamic processes, and outflows can redistribute it. Some disk winds and jets are strongly supported as magnetically driven, but their launching details and relative roles vary among systems and remain under investigation.

### Not established as universal rules

- Every protostar forms a large, long-lived Keplerian disk at the same stage.
- Every disk has the same angular-momentum transport mechanism.
- Every jet is launched from exactly the same radius.
- Every molecular outflow is a direct jet rather than entrained ambient material.
- Every observed streamer is feeding the central disk.
- Every accretion burst has the same trigger.
- Every outflow suppresses star formation, or every outflow triggers it.
- Every disk substructure is caused by a planet.
- A projected image alone supplies the complete three-dimensional flow.

## 17. Relationship audit: same mechanism, similar relationship, or similar appearance?

### Same mechanism

Angular-momentum conservation applies to both collapsing cores and circumstellar disks. The physical principle is the same, though the relevant scales and processes differ.

### Similar relationship, different mechanisms

Magnetic stresses, gravitational torques, and hydrodynamic transport can all redistribute angular momentum. They are not the same mechanism, even when they contribute to a comparable outcome.

### Coupled mechanisms

Disk accretion and outflow launching can be coupled through magnetic fields and angular-momentum transport. Accretion, ejection, and envelope interaction can occur simultaneously.

### Similar appearance only

Two bipolar structures may look alike but arise from different combinations of jet, wind, entrainment, and environmental geometry. A flattened continuum source may look disk-like without sufficient kinematic evidence to establish rotational support.

**Finding:** The system must be examined through its kinematics and physical conditions, not morphology alone.

## 24. Evidence update — 2026 observations and reviews

This section adds recent literature located during the continuation of the investigation. These sources refine the evidence base; they do not turn a result from one protostar into a universal rule.

### HOPS 358: resolved rotation in a protostellar disk wind

A 2026 *Nature Communications* study reports high-resolution ALMA observations of the Class 0 protostar HOPS 358. The authors identify a rotating, nested molecular outflow traced by H2CO, SO, and CH3OH. The outflow preserves the disk's rotational sense and is aligned with the disk axis. Their model comparison constrains a magnetic lever arm of approximately 2.3 and a wind-launching region at roughly 10–18 au.

The authors describe this as direct observational evidence for a magnetically launched disk wind in this particular system. The careful scope is important: the observed kinematics, interpreted with a stated wind model, support the MHD disk-wind explanation for HOPS 358. This does not demonstrate that every protostellar outflow has the same structure or that one mechanism dominates every disk.

Source: **Direct evidence for magnetohydrodynamic disk winds driving rotating outflows in protostar HOPS 358** (2026), *Nature Communications*.  
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-71142-3

### JOYS+: a broader JWST survey of young protostellar outflows

A 2026 *Astronomy & Astrophysics* JOYS+ study reports JWST/MIRI observations of a sample of single and multiple Class 0 and Class I protostars. It examines H2 wind and jet morphology, velocity and temperature structure, and mass outflow rates, together with ALMA CO maps.

A survey across multiple systems is valuable because it can test whether features found in individual case studies recur across a sample. Its derived quantities still depend on line excitation, geometry, extinction, source multiplicity, and the adopted analysis.

Source: Francis et al. (2026), **JOYS+: A JWST/MIRI survey of the evolution of H2 winds and jets from low-mass protostars**, *Astronomy & Astrophysics*, 711, A258.  
https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202558772

### Updated finding

The 2026 HOPS 358 result strengthens the observational case that a rotating MHD disk wind can remove angular momentum in at least one well-resolved Class 0 system. The JOYS+ survey extends the observational comparison across a larger sample. Together they make the accretion–ejection relationship a stronger research focus, while preserving the distinction between a source-specific detection, a sample-level trend, and a universal claim.
