# Investigation 014 — Cosmic-Ray Transport in the Magnetized Interstellar Medium: Propagation, Scattering, Diffusion, and Energy Loss

**Primary disciplines:** Astroparticle physics, cosmic-ray physics, plasma astrophysics, magnetohydrodynamics, high-energy astrophysics, gamma-ray astronomy, interstellar-medium physics.

**Investigation status:** Initial evidence-led investigation and Four-Question Study Method review recorded. The subject remains an active research problem; this record does not claim that one transport model applies at all energies, locations, or phases of the interstellar medium.

## 1. The question that initiated the investigation

Investigation 013 examined how magnetic fields participate in the geometry and dynamics of interstellar matter. It identified an important distinction: a magnetic field can influence charged-particle motion, but a field map does not reveal every particle trajectory or determine the complete transport process.

Investigation 014 follows that question:

**How do cosmic rays propagate through the magnetized interstellar medium, and how do magnetic geometry, turbulence, scattering, gas conditions, and particle energy shape their transport?**

A second question is essential:

**When particle motion appears diffusive at large scales, what local processes produce that large-scale description?**

These questions require several levels of description to remain distinct: individual particle motion, interactions with magnetic fluctuations, collective transport of a particle population, and the signals by which observers infer that transport.

## 2. Scientific terminology

- **Cosmic rays (CRs):** Energetic particles arriving from space, principally protons and atomic nuclei, with an electron and positron component. Galactic cosmic rays span a broad energy range. Their sources and propagation histories vary with energy and particle species.
- **Rigidity:** Momentum per unit electric charge, commonly expressed in GV or TV. Rigidity is often more useful than energy alone when comparing how different charged species respond to magnetic fields.
- **Larmor radius (gyroradius):** The characteristic radius of a charged particle's gyromotion around a magnetic field. In a uniform field, it depends on the particle's momentum perpendicular to the field and its charge. It generally increases with rigidity and decreases with magnetic-field strength.
- **Pitch angle:** The angle between a particle's momentum and the local magnetic-field direction.
- **Pitch-angle scattering:** Changes in particle pitch angle caused by interactions with magnetic fluctuations or other scattering processes. Repeated scattering can alter the net displacement of a particle population.
- **Streaming:** Directed transport of cosmic rays relative to the background plasma, often discussed along magnetic-field directions and in relation to gradients in cosmic-ray pressure.
- **Alfvén waves:** Magnetic and velocity perturbations that propagate through a conducting magnetized medium. Resonant interactions between particles and waves can scatter cosmic rays.
- **Self-confinement:** A model in which streaming cosmic rays excite waves that then scatter the cosmic rays, potentially regulating their own transport. Wave growth competes with damping mechanisms.
- **Extrinsic turbulence:** Pre-existing magnetic and plasma turbulence that scatters cosmic rays, rather than fluctuations generated primarily by the cosmic rays themselves.
- **Diffusion:** A large-scale statistical description of transport in which a particle population spreads down gradients. It is often represented by a diffusion coefficient or tensor. Diffusion is not necessarily the microscopic path followed by any individual particle.
- **Anisotropic diffusion:** Transport that differs by direction, often with distinct coefficients parallel and perpendicular to a mean magnetic field.
- **Advection:** Transport with the bulk motion of the background gas or plasma.
- **Field-line wandering:** Spatial wandering of magnetic-field lines due to turbulent magnetic structure. Particles moving along those lines can acquire substantial displacement across the large-scale mean field.
- **Cosmic-ray losses:** Processes that reduce particle energy or remove particles from a given energy range, including ionization and Coulomb losses, synchrotron radiation, inverse-Compton scattering, bremsstrahlung, and inelastic nuclear collisions. Which processes matter depends on species, energy, and environment.
- **Grammage:** The integrated mass column traversed by cosmic rays, often inferred from secondary-to-primary cosmic-ray abundance ratios. It is a transport diagnostic, not a direct measurement of geometric path length alone.

The vocabulary separates microscopic particle motion from population-level transport. A diffusion coefficient is an effective description of many trajectories under specified conditions; it is not a literal force or a universal constant of the Galaxy.

## 3. Why magnetic fields matter to cosmic rays

Cosmic rays are electrically charged, so their motion responds to electromagnetic fields. In many Galactic environments, their gyroradii are much smaller than the characteristic scales of the magnetic structures and turbulence they encounter. Their trajectories are therefore strongly affected by the magnetic environment.

This does not mean every particle follows one perfectly smooth field line. The field fluctuates; particles undergo gyromotion; waves and turbulence can change pitch angle; field lines themselves wander; and interactions with the background medium can alter particle energy.

NASA's educational account explains that Galactic, solar, and terrestrial magnetic fields deflect cosmic rays, scrambling arrival directions and making direct source identification difficult for many charged particles.

Source: NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, “Cosmic Rays.” https://imagine.gsfc.nasa.gov/science/toolbox/cosmic_rays1.html

## 4. Local motion and large-scale transport

### 4.1 Gyromotion and guiding-center motion

In a locally ordered magnetic field, a charged particle executes gyromotion around the field direction. Its guiding center can move along the field, while the particle's perpendicular motion is bounded by its gyroradius in the idealized uniform-field picture.

Real interstellar magnetic fields are neither perfectly uniform nor static. Gradients, curvature, fluctuations, and changes in plasma conditions complicate the simple picture. The uniform-field orbit is a starting model, not a complete description of Galactic propagation.

### 4.2 Pitch-angle scattering

Magnetic fluctuations can scatter particles in pitch angle. Repeated changes in pitch angle can reduce the persistence of a particle's directed motion along a field. At sufficiently large scales and times, a population may then be described by diffusion.

The effective scattering rate depends on the particle's rigidity, the spectrum and amplitude of magnetic fluctuations, the local plasma, and the relationship between the particle and the fluctuation scales. A single scattering rate should not be assumed for every energy or environment.

### 4.3 Streaming and self-generated waves

A cosmic-ray population with a spatial gradient can stream relative to the background plasma. Under suitable conditions, streaming can excite Alfvénic fluctuations. Those waves can scatter the particles, potentially reducing their net streaming speed and transferring energy and momentum to the plasma.

This is the basic self-confinement picture. It is conditional: wave growth must be compared with damping, including effects associated with ion-neutral collisions, nonlinear interactions, and other plasma processes. Where damping is strong, self-generated waves may not provide the dominant scattering.

### 4.4 Scattering by pre-existing turbulence

Cosmic rays can also scatter from magnetic fluctuations already present in the medium. This extrinsic-turbulence picture depends on the turbulent spectrum, the particle's resonant scales, and the geometry and strength of the magnetic field.

Self-confinement and extrinsic turbulence are not simply two names for the same mechanism. They identify different proposed sources of the fluctuations that scatter particles. Both may be relevant in different regimes, and simplified versions of either model can fail to reproduce observations.

A 2022 *MNRAS* study argued that standard forms of both models are incompatible with several observed cosmic-ray spectral features. Its result is a critique of those standard model implementations, not proof that wave generation or pre-existing turbulence never scatter cosmic rays.

Source: “Standard self-confinement and extrinsic turbulence models for cosmic ray transport are fundamentally incompatible with observations” (2022), *MNRAS* 517, 5413–5448. https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stac2909

### 4.5 Diffusion is an effective description

When many scattering events randomize particle directions sufficiently, the population's large-scale density can evolve approximately diffusively. In a magnetized environment, transport can be anisotropic. A diffusion tensor may distinguish motion parallel and perpendicular to the mean magnetic field.

However, diffusion is not guaranteed at every scale or time. Near a source, before sufficient scattering has occurred, transport may retain directional or non-diffusive features. Turbulent field geometry can also make the effective transport different from a simple homogeneous diffusion model.

A 2023 review examines cosmic-ray propagation in turbulent magnetic fields and the roles of turbulence, field-line wandering, and particle interactions.

Source: Lazarian, A., Xu, S. & Hu, Y. (2023), “Cosmic ray propagation in turbulent magnetic fields,” *Frontiers in Astronomy and Space Sciences* 10. https://doi.org/10.3389/fspas.2023.1154760

## 5. Transport is not necessarily the same in every interstellar phase

Investigation 011 described the ISM as multiphase. Cosmic-ray transport must be examined in that environment rather than in an assumed uniform gas.

Relevant differences include:

- **Warm ionized or partly ionized gas:** Ionization and plasma conditions affect wave propagation, damping, and particle coupling.
- **Warm neutral gas:** Charged-particle interactions with magnetic fluctuations remain important, but neutral material changes the damping and coupling environment.
- **Cold neutral and molecular regions:** Ion-neutral drift and damping can alter the survival of waves. Cosmic rays can still penetrate and ionize material, but their transport and energy losses depend on density, ionization, and particle energy.
- **Hot ionized plasma:** The plasma state, magnetic turbulence, and advection can produce transport conditions unlike those in dense neutral gas.

A 2025 study of spectrally resolved cosmic-ray transport in a dynamic, multiphase ISM simulation found different transport regimes in neutral and ionized regions. Its results indicate that advection and momentum-dependent diffusion can both contribute, with the balance varying by location and particle momentum.

Source: Armillotta, L., Ostriker, E. C. & Linzer, N. B. (2025), “Energy-Dependent Transport of Cosmic Rays in the Multiphase, Dynamic Interstellar Medium.” https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.00120

A 2025 *MNRAS* study developed a theory for cosmic-ray transport through inhomogeneous diffusive media. In its model, the long-time effective diffusion coefficient is the harmonic mean of the spatially varying local coefficients. This means low-diffusion regions can exert disproportionate influence on the overall transport. The result applies to the model studied; it should not be generalized to every Galactic transport problem without checking its assumptions.

Source: “Cosmic-ray transport in inhomogeneous media” (published online 13 December 2025), *MNRAS* 545, Issue 2 (2026), staf2108. https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/staf2108

A 2026 study of PeV cosmic rays in a turbulent multiphase ISM used three-dimensional MHD simulations and test-particle calculations. It reported that phase boundaries can create localized magnetic fluctuations and scattering, while the volume-filling warm and unstable phases dominate global transport in the simulated conditions. The authors' numerical findings are specific to their modeled phases, energy range, and setup; they do not establish a universal transport coefficient for the Galaxy.

Source: Hu, Y. (2026), “Diffusion of PeV Cosmic Rays in the Turbulent and Multiphase Interstellar Medium,” *Galaxies* 14(2), 33. https://doi.org/10.3390/galaxies14020033

## 6. Energy changes and particle losses

Transport is not only a question of where particles move. Their energy can change during propagation.

- **Ionization and Coulomb losses** can be important for lower-energy particles passing through gas and plasma.
- **Synchrotron losses** affect relativistic electrons and positrons as they radiate in magnetic fields.
- **Inverse-Compton losses** occur when energetic electrons transfer energy to photons.
- **Bremsstrahlung** occurs when charged particles are deflected in the electric fields of nuclei and other charged particles.
- **Inelastic proton-proton collisions** can produce secondary particles, including charged and neutral pions; neutral-pion decay produces gamma rays.
- **Adiabatic energy changes** can occur when cosmic rays interact with expanding or compressing flows.

The importance of a loss process depends on the particle species, energy, gas density, radiation field, magnetic-field strength, and residence time. Electrons and protons at the same nominal energy do not have the same loss history.

The transport problem therefore involves coupled questions: how long particles remain in a region, what matter and radiation they encounter, how they lose energy, and how their distribution changes.

## 7. How transport is investigated observationally

### 7.1 Direct cosmic-ray measurements

Detectors near Earth measure cosmic-ray energy spectra, elemental and isotopic composition, and arrival-direction anisotropy. Secondary-to-primary abundance ratios can constrain the amount of matter traversed and the residence history of cosmic rays.

These measurements are valuable but are made after particles have propagated through the Galaxy and the heliosphere. Solar modulation affects lower-energy cosmic rays, and magnetic deflection means the arrival direction of a charged particle may not point back to its source.

### 7.2 Gamma-ray observations

Cosmic-ray protons and nuclei can produce gamma rays through collisions with gas. Cosmic-ray electrons can produce gamma rays through inverse-Compton scattering and bremsstrahlung. Gamma-ray maps therefore provide indirect information about cosmic-ray populations and their interactions with the environment.

The inference is not unique. Gas distributions, radiation fields, source histories, particle spectra, and line-of-sight integration must be considered. A gamma-ray brightness map is not a direct photograph of cosmic-ray trajectories.

### 7.3 Extended gamma-ray halos

Extended gamma-ray emission around nearby energetic sources can constrain the propagation of cosmic-ray electrons and positrons. The morphology and spectrum of the emission depend on particle injection, transport, magnetic and radiation environments, and energy losses.

The HAWC observations around Geminga have been used to constrain cosmic-ray transport and magnetic turbulence in the local environment. A 2018 analysis found that the data were compatible with particular turbulent-field models while implying a diffusion coefficient lower than values commonly used in Galactic propagation codes. This illustrates that transport can vary by environment and that a local source region need not represent the entire Galaxy.

Source: “Constraining the properties of the magnetic turbulence in the Geminga region using HAWC gamma-ray data” (2018), *MNRAS* 479, 4526–4534. https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/sty1821

### 7.4 Anisotropy and the limits of source tracing

Because magnetic fields deflect charged cosmic rays, their arrival directions can be strongly scrambled. Large-scale anisotropy still carries information about gradients and transport, but it does not provide a simple one-to-one map from arrival direction to source.

Gamma rays travel without the same magnetic deflection and can therefore provide a more direct directional connection to their production regions, although the gamma-ray production process and line-of-sight emission still require interpretation.

## 9. Relationship audit

### Same mechanism

Pitch-angle scattering by magnetic fluctuations is a physical mechanism that can contribute to the effective diffusion of cosmic-ray populations. Where the relevant scattering assumptions hold, microscopic interactions and the macroscopic transport description can be connected through a kinetic or transport model.

### Comparable relationship

The movement of particles through a magnetized, turbulent environment and the movement of matter through the same environment can both be shaped by field geometry and fluctuations. This is a comparable relationship, not an assertion that cosmic rays and ordinary gas share identical transport mechanisms. Cosmic rays are non-thermal energetic particles; the background gas follows its own fluid and kinetic processes.

### Analogy

Describing field lines as pathways can help communicate why magnetic geometry matters. It is only an analogy if it suggests that every particle is permanently confined to one fixed line. Turbulence, scattering, field-line wandering, and cross-field transport prevent that simplified picture from being universal.

### Unresolved comparison

A region with reduced cosmic-ray diffusion may appear to act as a transport bottleneck. Whether a particular cloud boundary, ionization transition, or magnetic interface produces that effect must be demonstrated from the local physical conditions and observations. A visible boundary is not automatically a transport barrier.

## 10. Boundary and participation examination

The boundary question is especially important in cosmic-ray transport because the ISM is multiphase and magnetically structured.

- **Magnetic interfaces:** Changes in field strength or orientation can affect particle trajectories, mirroring, and scattering. The effect depends on particle rigidity and the detailed field configuration.
- **Ionization boundaries:** A transition between ionized and neutral material can change wave damping and particle–plasma coupling. This can alter the conditions for self-generated scattering.
- **Cloud boundaries:** Density and composition changes can affect energy losses and gamma-ray production. A cloud may be a target for cosmic-ray interactions without being an impermeable barrier to cosmic rays.
- **Turbulent interfaces:** Fluctuations can change field-line geometry and scattering. Their influence may extend beyond a sharply defined physical boundary.
- **Observational boundaries:** A telescope beam, energy threshold, or line-of-sight integration is a limit of the measurement, not necessarily a physical interface in the ISM.

A boundary may therefore separate regions, participate in exchange, alter transport conditions, or exist only in the investigator's measurement procedure. The actual role must be established for each case.

## 13. What is established, inferred, and unresolved?

### Established by the cited scientific literature

- Galactic cosmic rays are charged particles whose trajectories are affected by magnetic fields.
- Magnetic fluctuations can scatter cosmic rays and influence their transport.
- Diffusion is a useful large-scale description in many regimes, but it is not necessarily the local motion of an individual particle.
- Transport can be anisotropic, energy-dependent, and sensitive to the multiphase structure of the ISM.
- Cosmic-ray energy losses depend on particle species, energy, and environment.
- Direct cosmic-ray measurements and indirect gamma-ray observations constrain different aspects of propagation.

### Supported inference

The magnetic and plasma environment can be described as part of the framework and constraint conditions shaping cosmic-ray propagation. The observed transport is a manifestation reconstructed from multiple witnesses and interpreted through physical models.

This is a descriptive placement supported by the cited science, not an independent prediction or proof supplied by GUA-W terminology.

### Unresolved

- Under what conditions does self-confinement dominate over scattering by pre-existing turbulence?
- How do cosmic-ray transport coefficients vary across the hot, warm, cold, and molecular phases?
- How much cross-field transport results from field-line wandering, local scattering, magnetic mirrors, or other processes?
- How do cosmic-ray transport and energy losses change near cloud boundaries and ionization transitions?
- Which observations can distinguish streaming, diffusion, advection, and non-diffusive transport in a particular source environment?
- How should transport models reconcile local gamma-ray halos with Galaxy-wide cosmic-ray spectra and anisotropy?
- How do cosmic rays transfer energy and momentum to the ISM, and under which conditions does that feedback materially change gas dynamics?

These questions remain open. The investigation does not select one transport model as universally correct.
