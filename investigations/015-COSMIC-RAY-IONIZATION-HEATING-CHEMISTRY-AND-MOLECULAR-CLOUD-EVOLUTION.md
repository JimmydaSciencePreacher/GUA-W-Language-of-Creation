# Investigation 015 — Cosmic-Ray Influence on Molecular Clouds: Ionization, Heating, Chemistry, and Cloud Evolution

**Primary disciplines:** Astrochemistry, molecular-cloud physics, cosmic-ray physics, plasma astrophysics, star-formation physics, infrared and radio astronomy.

**Investigation status:** Initial evidence-led investigation and Four-Question Study Method review recorded. The investigation distinguishes established interaction pathways from the still-active question of how strongly cosmic rays influence the evolution of any particular cloud.

## 1. The question that initiated the investigation

Investigation 014 examined how cosmic rays propagate through the magnetized interstellar medium. It identified that energetic particles can penetrate regions shielded from ordinary ultraviolet starlight and interact with gas, dust, and molecules.

The next question is:

**What happens to a molecular cloud when cosmic rays ionize and deposit energy into its material, and how do those interactions influence chemistry, temperature, magnetic coupling, and cloud evolution?**

A second question follows:

**Can the observed chemical and thermal state of a cloud be used to infer the cosmic-ray environment that acted upon it?**

These questions must not be collapsed into the claim that cosmic rays determine every stage of star formation. Their influence varies with particle energy, cloud column density, gas density, local sources, magnetic transport, and the physical and chemical history of the cloud.

## 2. Scientific terminology

- **Cosmic-ray ionization rate (CRIR), ζ:** The rate at which cosmic rays produce ionization events per target particle, commonly quoted per H₂ molecule or per hydrogen atom. The convention and target species must be stated when comparing values.
- **Primary ionization:** Ionization caused directly by an energetic cosmic-ray particle interacting with an atom or molecule.
- **Secondary ionization:** Additional ionization caused by energetic electrons released in primary interactions and by subsequent particle cascades.
- **Dissociation:** Breaking a molecular bond. Cosmic-ray interactions and their secondary products can initiate dissociation as well as ionization.
- **Ion–molecule chemistry:** Chemical reactions between ions and neutral molecules. Many reactions proceed rapidly in cold interstellar gas and are initiated or sustained by ionization.
- **H₃⁺:** The molecular hydrogen ion formed through a sequence beginning with cosmic-ray ionization of H₂. It is a central intermediate in diffuse-cloud ion chemistry and an observational tracer used to constrain ionization conditions.
- **Cosmic-ray-induced ultraviolet (CRUV) field:** Ultraviolet photons generated inside shielded gas through excitation processes associated with cosmic-ray interactions. This can drive photochemistry where external ultraviolet radiation is strongly attenuated.
- **Cosmic-ray heating:** Transfer of part of the energy deposited by energetic particles and secondary electrons into the thermal motion of gas particles.
- **Ionization fraction:** The fraction of particles or gas constituents in an ionized state. The precise definition depends on whether the quantity refers to electrons, a particular ion, or total charged species.
- **Ambipolar diffusion:** Relative motion between charged components tied to magnetic fields and neutral gas. Ionization chemistry can therefore influence magnetic coupling in partly ionized molecular material.
- **Column density:** The number of particles per unit area along a line of sight. It is important for evaluating attenuation and the penetration of low-energy cosmic rays through a cloud.
- **Chemical network:** A set of chemical species and reactions used to calculate or model the evolution of abundances under specified physical conditions.
- **Chemical tracer:** An observed molecule or molecular ratio used to infer physical or chemical conditions. A tracer is not a direct measurement of every process that affects it.

## 3. From cosmic-ray impact to molecular chemistry

### 3.1 Ionization of molecular hydrogen

In shielded molecular material, external ultraviolet photons may be strongly attenuated. Cosmic rays can still penetrate much of the gas and ionize molecular hydrogen.

A simplified initiating sequence is:

1. A cosmic-ray particle ionizes H₂.
2. The resulting H₂⁺ reacts rapidly with another H₂ molecule.
3. H₃⁺ is produced.
4. H₃⁺ transfers a proton to other molecules or participates in dissociative recombination.
5. A network of ion–molecule reactions develops.

The sequence is simplified: the actual reaction network includes branching, secondary electrons, different species, and environment-dependent reaction rates. It nevertheless demonstrates why ionization can influence chemistry even in cold, externally shielded regions.

Indriolo and McCall's review describes cosmic-ray ionization as a driver of interstellar ion–molecule chemistry and reviews observables used to infer cosmic-ray conditions.

Source: Indriolo, N. & McCall, B. J. (2013), “Cosmic-ray astrochemistry,” *Chemical Society Reviews* 42, 7763–7773. https://doi.org/10.1039/c3cs60087d

### 3.2 Secondary electrons and chemical pathways

A primary ionization event does not end with the first charged products. Energetic secondary electrons can cause further ionization, excitation, and dissociation. Their energy is partitioned among several channels.

Consequently, a cloud's chemical response depends not only on the number of primary cosmic-ray interactions but also on the energy distribution of the particles and the subsequent interaction cascade.

The total effect cannot be represented accurately by assuming that every ionization event produces one identical chemical outcome.

### 3.3 Cosmic-ray-induced ultraviolet chemistry

Cosmic-ray interactions can excite H₂ and other species. Subsequent radiative transitions produce ultraviolet photons within the cloud. These photons can drive chemical reactions in regions where direct stellar ultraviolet radiation is weak.

This is an important distinction: a cloud can be shielded from external ultraviolet radiation without being chemically inactive. Internal energy deposition can sustain reactions in dark regions.

The strength and consequences of this internally generated radiation depend on the cosmic-ray ionization rate, gas composition, dust attenuation, and chemical network.

## 4. Heating and the thermal state of a cloud

Cosmic rays can heat gas through energy transfer from energetic particles and secondary electrons. The heating rate depends on the ionization rate and on how much energy per ionization is converted into heat under the relevant conditions.

Heating competes with cooling processes, including molecular line emission, atomic fine-structure emission, gas–dust energy exchange, and other environment-dependent channels.

The gas temperature is therefore not determined by cosmic-ray heating alone. It emerges from the balance of heating and cooling processes.

This distinction matters when examining a cold dense core. A measured temperature cannot be attributed to cosmic rays without evaluating other heating sources, cooling channels, density, dust coupling, and the region's history.

## 5. Chemistry, tracers, and what observations actually establish

### 5.1 H₃⁺ in diffuse molecular clouds

H₃⁺ absorption lines can provide a useful constraint on the cosmic-ray ionization rate in diffuse molecular gas. The inference depends on the observed column densities, hydrogen abundance, electron abundance, chemical assumptions, and the physical distribution of material along the sight line.

A 2025 preprint by Indriolo and collaborators reports H₃⁺ absorption observations in 12 of 27 targeted diffuse-cloud sight lines. Their modeling gives a combined sample mean H₂ ionization rate of approximately 5.3 × 10⁻¹⁷ s⁻¹, with a reported standard deviation of 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁷ s⁻¹. The authors construct a sparsely sampled three-dimensional map within roughly 1 kpc and report variations by factors of about five over scales near 100 pc.

This is a preprint result, not a final universal value for the Milky Way. It is valuable because it illustrates spatial variation and the need to preserve the distinction between a sample estimate and a Galaxy-wide constant.

Source: Indriolo, N. et al. (2025), “Mapping the Cosmic-Ray Ionization Rate in the Local Galaxy with H₃⁺,” arXiv:2511.13915. https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.13915

### 5.2 Dense cores and molecular tracers

In dense cold cores, direct H₃⁺ measurements can be difficult. Researchers therefore use other molecular ions and abundance ratios, including H₂D⁺, DCO⁺, HCO⁺, and CO-related measurements, together with chemical models.

A 2025 *Astronomy & Astrophysics* study estimated cosmic-ray ionization rates in 17 low-mass cores. The reported values span approximately 1.3 × 10⁻¹⁸ to 8.5 × 10⁻¹⁷ s⁻¹. The study found no significant correlation with core H₂ column density in its sample, but did report a positive correlation with estimated core temperature.

This is a sample-specific result. It does not establish that temperature universally controls ionization; environmental conditions and the assumptions used to infer the ionization rate remain important.

Source: “Cosmic-ray ionisation rate in low-mass cores: The role of the environment” (2025), *Astronomy & Astrophysics*. https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202453198

### 5.3 Chemical abundance is an indirect witness

Molecular abundances reflect multiple influences: initial elemental abundances, temperature, density, depletion onto dust grains, radiation, chemical age, gas motion, and reaction rates.

A molecule's abundance can be consistent with a particular cosmic-ray environment without uniquely proving that environment. Chemical modeling is required to evaluate competing explanations.

The investigator must therefore distinguish:
- measured spectral line intensity;
- inferred molecular column density;
- modeled molecular abundance;
- inferred cosmic-ray ionization rate;
- interpretation of the cloud's history.

These are successive evidentiary steps, not interchangeable observations.

## 6. Attenuation and the cloud interior

Cosmic rays have a broad energy distribution. Lower-energy particles generally lose energy more readily through interactions with matter, so their penetration and contribution to ionization can vary with the column density traversed.

A cloud does not necessarily experience one uniform ionization rate from edge to center. The local particle spectrum may change with depth, and transport can depend on magnetic geometry, scattering, and the cloud's environment.

A 2022 study of dense-core chemistry modeled cosmic-ray ionization, dissociation, and heating while examining attenuation through dense material. It emphasizes that cosmic-ray effects and chemical evolution depend on the column density and physical conditions of the core.

Source: O'Donoghue, R. et al. (2022), “The Effects of Cosmic Rays on the Chemistry of Dense Cores,” *The Astrophysical Journal* 934, 63. https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ac7963

A 2024 analysis by the Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics reported revised estimates of the local cosmic-ray ionization rate in nearby diffuse molecular clouds, finding values about an order of magnitude below some earlier estimates. This demonstrates that inferred rates can change when cloud structure, dust distribution, and modeling assumptions are improved.

Source: Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics (2024), “The cosmic-ray ionization rate in the local Milky Way is ten times lower than previously thought.” https://www.mpe.mpg.de/8032858/news20240930

## 7. A useful counterexample: the Galactic centre

A strong investigation must examine cases where an attractive explanation does not adequately account for the observations.

A 2025 *Astronomy & Astrophysics* study examined high ionization rates inferred in the Central Molecular Zone of the Galactic centre. The authors tested cosmic-ray spectra against gamma-ray and radio constraints and concluded that cosmic rays alone could not explain the observed high ionization rates under the models they examined.

This is important. Cosmic rays are a major ionization source in many interstellar environments, but the existence of a high ionization rate does not automatically mean cosmic rays alone explain it. Other energetic processes and environmental conditions must be evaluated.

Source: Ravikularaman, S., Recchia, S., Phan, V. H. M. & Gabici, S. (2025), “Cosmic rays cannot explain the high ionisation rates in the Galactic centre,” *Astronomy & Astrophysics* 694, A114. https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202451155

## 8. Current research: high-energy astrochemistry

A review published in January 2026 describes the growing field of high-energy astrochemistry, combining cosmic-ray and X-ray processes with laboratory studies of energetic-particle interactions in astrophysical ices.

The review discusses nonthermal processes in both gas and solid phases, including the chemistry of cold dense regions where ordinary external ultraviolet radiation is strongly attenuated.

This widens the investigation beyond gas-phase ion–molecule reactions. Energetic particles can also affect ice chemistry on dust grains. However, cosmic rays and X-rays are distinct energy sources and their contributions must not be conflated.

Source: Gaches, B. & Viti, S. (2026), “High-Energy Astrochemistry in the Molecular Interstellar Medium,” *ACS Earth and Space Chemistry* 10(2), 276–300. https://doi.org/10.1021/acsearthspacechem.5c00304

## 10. Relationship audit

### Same mechanism

Cosmic-ray ionization of H₂ initiates a chemical sequence that can produce H₃⁺ and subsequent ion–molecule reactions. The connection between ionization and the resulting reaction network is a physical chemical mechanism supported by laboratory reaction data and astronomical observations.

### Comparable relationship

Cosmic-ray energy deposition and other heating sources can both contribute to the thermal state of a cloud. They are comparable in their contribution to an energy balance, but their microscopic mechanisms are different.

### Analogy

A chemical network may be described as a connected framework of possible reactions. This is a useful analogy for organizing relationships, but the diagram is not the chemical process itself. Actual reaction rates, concentrations, and physical conditions determine which pathways operate.

### Unresolved comparison

A spatial gradient in molecular abundance and a gradient in cosmic-ray ionization may appear related. Their relationship must be tested against temperature, density, depletion, radiation, chemical age, and transport. Similar spatial patterns do not establish that cosmic rays caused the abundance gradient.

## 11. Boundary and participation examination

- **Cloud surface:** External radiation and particles encounter changing column density and composition. The surface may affect penetration, but the cloud is not necessarily a sealed barrier.
- **Interior column:** Material accumulated along the path can attenuate lower-energy cosmic rays and alter the spectrum reaching deeper regions.
- **Gas–dust interface:** Gas-phase molecules can freeze onto grains, while energetic processing and thermal or nonthermal desorption can return species to the gas. The grain surface participates in chemical exchange.
- **Ion–neutral relationship:** Charged particles and neutral gas exchange momentum through collisions. Ionization conditions therefore influence magnetic coupling and ambipolar diffusion.
- **Chemical phase boundary:** A transition in temperature, density, or composition may change which reactions dominate. The transition can be physical, gradual, or defined by an observational threshold.
- **Measurement boundary:** A telescope beam and a line of sight combine signals from multiple locations. This is a boundary of the measurement, not necessarily a sharp boundary in the cloud.

The boundary question has no universal answer. Some interfaces participate in exchange and transformation; others are analytical divisions. The evidence must identify the particular case.

## 14. What is established, inferred, and unresolved?

### Established by the cited scientific literature

- Cosmic rays ionize atoms and molecules in interstellar gas.
- Ionization initiates chemical pathways, including the formation of H₃⁺ and subsequent ion–molecule reactions.
- Cosmic-ray interactions can contribute to gas heating, dissociation, excitation, and internally generated ultraviolet chemistry.
- Cosmic-ray effects can occur in regions shielded from external ultraviolet radiation.
- Molecular abundances and spectral lines can be used to infer ionization conditions, but the inference depends on chemical and physical models.
- Ionization rates vary across environments and are not adequately represented by one universal value.
- Cosmic rays alone do not explain every observed high-ionization environment.

### Supported inference

Cosmic rays are one physical contributor to the chemical and thermal conditions of molecular clouds. Their influence can extend into shielded regions and can affect ionization fractions relevant to magnetic coupling. The magnitude of that influence depends on local particle spectra, cloud structure, and competing processes.

### Unresolved

- How does the low-energy cosmic-ray spectrum vary from diffuse cloud surfaces to dense core interiors?
- How much of the observed variation in ionization rate is caused by local particle sources, attenuation, transport, or chemical-model uncertainty?
- Under which conditions does cosmic-ray heating dominate over other heating processes?
- How do energetic particles alter the chemistry of dust-grain ice mantles at different temperatures and radiation levels?
- How strongly does cosmic-ray-driven ionization alter magnetic coupling and ambipolar diffusion in individual cores?
- Can observations distinguish cosmic-ray-driven chemistry from X-ray-driven or other nonthermal chemical pathways?
- Under what conditions does cosmic-ray energy deposition materially alter cloud evolution or star formation rather than act as one contribution among many?

These questions remain open. No universal cosmic-ray control of molecular-cloud evolution is asserted.

Cosmic rays have now been examined as particles that propagate through the ISM and interact with the chemistry and thermal state of molecular clouds.
