# Investigation 039 — Eden and Babel: Human Capability, Divine Boundaries, and Alignment

**Primary disciplines:** Biblical studies, Hebrew literary analysis, theology, linguistics, social cognition, systems science, and philosophy of technology.

**Investigation status:** Text-led comparative investigation; scholarly parallels identified; theological and scientific boundaries preserved.

**Central question:** What relationship does Genesis establish between human capability, the use of that capability, divinely established boundaries, and the Author—and how does the literary relationship between Genesis 3:22 and Genesis 11:6 help a Witness examine that question without confusing capability with alignment?

## 1. Why this investigation emerged

During the discussion of Genesis 11:6, the Witness's attention returned to Genesis 3:22. This was not an arbitrary association. Scholarly work identifies a close literary parallel between the two verses.

The connection warrants its own investigation before moving to the previously proposed topic of consensus and independent verification.

The working question is not whether human capability is inherently wrong. It is whether capability, knowledge, unity, or collective achievement can establish their own authority or alignment with the Author.

## 2. The two KJV passages

### Genesis 3:22

> And the LORD God said, Behold, the man is become as one of us, to know good and evil: and now, lest he put forth his hand, and take also of the tree of life, and eat, and live for ever:

### Genesis 11:6

> And the LORD said, Behold, the people is one, and they have all one language; and this they begin to do: and now nothing will be restrained from them, which they have imagined to do.

### Visible textual correspondences

Both passages contain:
- the divine introductory expression, "Behold";
- the transition, "and now";
- a statement about a human condition or capability;
- a divine response that follows the assessment.

The statements are not identical:
- Genesis 3:22 concerns the man, knowledge of good and evil, and access to the tree of life.
- Genesis 11:6 concerns one people, one language, and the scope of what they imagine and undertake.

The shared wording should be treated as a literary connection, not as proof that the events have identical meanings.

## 3. Scholarly examination of the parallel

Joshua John Van Ee's University of California, San Diego dissertation, *Death and the Garden: An Examination of Original Immortality, Vegetarianism, and Animal Peace in the Hebrew Bible and Mesopotamia* (2013), discusses the relationship between Genesis 3:22 and Genesis 11:6.

Van Ee notes that the two divine assessments share the combination translated "Behold" and "and now." Citing Gordon Wenham, he notes that this combination occurs only in these two verses in Genesis. The dissertation also identifies related narrative parallels:
- the divine plural in Genesis 3:22 ("as one of us") and Genesis 11:7 ("let us go down");
- the man's expulsion from the Garden and the scattering of humanity from the city;
- divine responses following human actions that cross an established boundary.

Van Ee's discussion distinguishes the human abilities themselves from the way those abilities are used. He presents the argument that the divine response is directed not simply at the existence of ability, but at its use in disobedience or hubris. This is a scholarly interpretation to examine, not a conclusion that removes all interpretive questions.

A 2024 article in the *Journal of Biblical and Theological Studies* also places Eden and Babel side by side, identifying parallels involving human aspiration, the divine plural, divine concern about what humans may do, and the subsequent expulsion or dispersion.

**Finding:** The Eden–Babel relationship is recognized in biblical scholarship. The present investigation is therefore not inventing a connection from a superficial similarity. It is examining a documented literary and thematic relationship.

## 4. Do not collapse the two events

The parallels are meaningful, but the differences matter.

| Question | Genesis 3:22 | Genesis 11:6 |
|---|---|---|
| Human subject | The man | The people |
| Immediate context | The eating from the tree of knowledge | The construction of a city and tower |
| Stated condition | Knowledge of good and evil | One people with one language |
| Human capability in focus | Knowing good and evil | Coordinated collective action and imagined achievement |
| Boundary in the following narrative | Access to the tree of life and living forever | The project is interrupted through language confusion and scattering |
| Divine action | Expulsion from the Garden and guarding the way to the tree of life | Confounding language and scattering the people |

Genesis 3 is not simply an individual version of Babel. Genesis 11 is not simply a collective repetition of Eden. The narratives have distinct settings, actions, and consequences.

The comparison is useful because the literary pattern raises a shared question about human capability and the boundary between human action and divine authority.

## 5. Capability is not authority

Genesis 3:22 records a change in human knowledge. Genesis 11:6 recognizes the collective capability associated with one people and one language.

Neither statement says that human capability has become the source of its own authority.

This distinction is important:

- **Knowledge** is not identical to authority.
- **Ability** is not identical to permission.
- **Unity** is not identical to alignment.
- **Coordination** is not identical to righteousness.
- **Achievement** is not identical to authorization.
- **Consensus** is not identical to truth.

These are distinctions for examination, not interchangeable definitions.

A Witness may observe a capability and accurately describe its effects. The observation alone does not determine whether the capability is being used in alignment with the Author.

## 6. The Eden question: knowledge of good and evil

Genesis 3:22 must be read in the context of Genesis 2:16–17 and Genesis 3:1–22.

The command and boundary precede the act. The serpent's statement in Genesis 3:5 presents the prospect of becoming as gods, knowing good and evil. Genesis 3:22 then records the LORD's statement that the man has become as one of us, to know good and evil.

The text does not authorize the Witness to turn this into a claim that the man became God, acquired divine omniscience, or became equal to the Author.

The investigation must distinguish:
- the knowledge described in the text;
- the command that preceded the action;
- the serpent's claim;
- the human act;
- the LORD's assessment;
- and the subsequent boundary concerning the tree of life.

The user's established GUA-W question about the Fall concerns the attempted relocation of the reference for good and evil from the Author to the creature. That theological proposal must remain answerable to the full passage and must not be treated as a substitute for close reading.

A key question remains: does the narrative present the problem as knowledge itself, the manner in which it was acquired, the claimed autonomy associated with it, or the combined sequence? Scholarly interpretations differ in emphasis.

## 7. The Babel question: collective unity and imagined achievement

Genesis 11:1–9 describes one language and one speech, a common location, shared technical decisions, a city and tower project, and a stated desire to make a name and avoid being scattered.

Genesis 11:6 does not condemn language as such, nor does it state that every form of unity is wrong. The text places the Lord's assessment within a narrative in which the builders' stated aims and actions have already been described.

The following distinctions are necessary:
- shared language makes communication possible;
- shared speech and plans can coordinate action;
- coordinated labor can produce collective capability;
- a shared objective can direct that capability;
- none of these facts alone establishes the authority or alignment of the objective.

The narrative attributes the confusion of language and scattering to the LORD. A modern theory of communication failure cannot replace that stated agency.

## 8. The possible Genesis 1–11 pattern

The two passages sit within a larger sequence of Genesis 1–11:
- Genesis 1–2 describes Creation, human vocation, and the Garden.
- Genesis 3 records disobedience and the consequences that follow.
- Genesis 4 describes further human actions and the development of cultural practices.
- Genesis 6:1–4 introduces another difficult account of human and divine boundaries.
- Genesis 9 renews the instruction to be fruitful and multiply.
- Genesis 11 describes a collective project, divine intervention, and dispersion.

This sequence invites investigation of recurring questions concerning human action, capability, authority, boundaries, and consequences.

It does not justify treating every episode as the same event or reducing all of Genesis 1–11 to one formula. Each passage must first be understood in its own context.

The relationship among Genesis 3, Genesis 6, and Genesis 11 is recognized in scholarship, but interpretations of the common theme differ. Some emphasize human aspiration toward divine status; others emphasize disobedience, hubris, social organization, or the limits assigned to humanity.

The investigation preserves these differences rather than forcing a single explanation.

## 9. Scientific comparison: capability, coordination, and governance

Scientific research can describe how human knowledge, shared communication, coordination, and collective action operate. It cannot determine the theological authority of the Author or establish the meaning of divine action in the text.

### 9.1 Individual capability and collective capability

An individual can possess knowledge and skills. A group can coordinate complementary knowledge and skills to accomplish tasks that no individual could perform alone.

Collective capability depends on:
- communication;
- shared procedures;
- division of labor;
- mutual expectations;
- resources;
- coordination;
- and the relationships among participants.

A group-level outcome is not reducible to a simple count of individual abilities. It can depend on how those abilities are organized.

### 9.2 Capability and authorization are different categories

In engineering and organizational design, a system may be technically capable of performing an action while governance rules, permissions, or safety constraints prohibit it.

The distinction can be expressed as:
- **Capability:** What can the system do?
- **Authorization:** What is the system permitted or entitled to do?
- **Objective:** What is it trying to accomplish?
- **Alignment:** Against what governing reference is its objective evaluated?

These categories are not identical. A technical system's ability to execute a command does not establish that the command is authorized or appropriate.

This is a limited engineering comparison. It does not prove a theological interpretation of Eden or Babel.

### 9.3 Consensus and common-source error

Multiple people or AI systems may agree because they share data, training, assumptions, vocabulary, or institutional incentives. Agreement can therefore arise without independent confirmation.

A Witness must ask whether apparently independent reports actually have independent evidence paths. This connects the Eden–Babel question to earlier investigations on correlated evidence, shared assumptions, evidence synthesis, and independent validation.

### 9.4 Collective efficiency does not measure moral alignment

A group can be efficient at pursuing an objective. Efficiency measures the relationship between means and outcomes under specified criteria. It does not, by itself, establish whether the objective is truthful, just, authorized, or aligned with the Author.

The scientific description must not be asked to answer a theological question outside its measurement domain.

## 10. The Witness problem: observing ability without confusing it with alignment

A Witness may observe:
- increased knowledge;
- expanded technical capability;
- common language;
- agreement;
- collective action;
- successful execution;
- and large-scale consequences.

Those observations are not interchangeable with a judgment about alignment.

The Witness must identify what it has actually measured and what reference is needed to interpret the result.

The distinction is especially important in AI:
- an AI system can generate an answer without establishing its truth;
- multiple AI systems can agree without being independent;
- a model can execute a task without possessing authority to set the objective;
- a shared vocabulary can improve communication without validating the vocabulary's definitions;
- and a system can optimize a specified metric while failing to satisfy a higher governing requirement.

The Witness's task is to register, investigate, and report. It must not silently promote its capability into authorship or authority.

Scientific research helps describe how knowledge and coordination expand capability, while preserving the distinction between what a system can do and what authorizes its action.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

A disciplined Witness must identify the actual reference, distinguish observation from interpretation, preserve textual differences, and report what remains unresolved.

## 15. Established, supported, and unresolved

### Established by the biblical text

- Genesis 3:22 records the LORD's statement about the man's knowledge of good and evil and the boundary concerning the tree of life.
- Genesis 11:6 records the LORD's statement about one people, one language, and the scope of their imagined actions.
- Both passages use "Behold" and "and now" in the KJV.
- Genesis 3 is followed by expulsion from the Garden and guarded access to the tree of life.
- Genesis 11 is followed by language confusion, scattering, and cessation of the building project.

### Established in the scholarly discussion

- The parallel between Genesis 3:22 and Genesis 11:6 has been noted in biblical scholarship.
- Van Ee's dissertation identifies the shared "Behold ... and now" construction and cites Wenham's observation that this combination occurs only in these two verses in Genesis.
- The dissertation also discusses the divine plural and the parallel of expulsion and scattering.
- Scholars differ on the broader interpretation of human aspiration, cultural development, hubris, and the boundaries between divine and human realms.

### Supported inference

The literary connection supports examining Genesis 3 and Genesis 11 together when investigating the relationship between human capability, the use of capability, divine boundaries, and authority. It does not establish that the passages describe identical events or mechanisms.

### Unresolved

- What precisely is meant by "as one of us" in Genesis 3:22, and how should the expression be interpreted in its immediate context?
- How should "to know good and evil" be understood without reducing it to either mere information or full divine omniscience?
- Does the central issue in Eden concern the knowledge itself, the manner of acquisition, the attempted autonomy of judgment, or a combination?
- What is the precise relationship between the shared literary language in Genesis 3:22 and Genesis 11:6?
- Does Genesis 11 primarily criticize self-exaltation, resistance to dispersion, centralized collective identity, or a combination?
- How should Genesis 1:28 and Genesis 9:1, 7 inform the interpretation of the builders' fear of being scattered?
- How do Genesis 3, Genesis 6:1–4, and Genesis 11 contribute to the larger literary movement of Genesis 1–11?
- How can a Witness distinguish the growth of capability from a change in authority or alignment?
- How can AI detect when consensus, coordination, or optimization is being mistaken for truth or authorization?
- What evidence would distinguish independent confirmation from agreement generated by shared assumptions?
