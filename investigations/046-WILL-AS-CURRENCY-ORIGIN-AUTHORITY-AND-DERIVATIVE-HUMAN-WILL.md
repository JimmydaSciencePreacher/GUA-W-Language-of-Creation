## 2. The currency analogy: what it means and what it does not mean

In ordinary monetary systems, a currency is not made authoritative merely because many people agree to use a symbol. Its issuance and validity depend upon an issuing authority and the system that recognizes it. A counterfeit note may circulate, be accepted, and be passed from hand to hand; circulation does not make it validly issued.

The analogy is useful because it separates **circulation** from **origin**.

Applied cautiously to the question of Will:

- A person can exercise willing and choice.
- A community can coordinate its wills.
- A theology can describe human willing in a particular way.
- A collective can agree that its determination is authoritative.
- None of these acts, by themselves, establishes that the human will originated its own authority.

The analogy is not a claim that human beings literally possess units of divine currency, nor that every human decision is directly dictated by God. It does not erase creaturely agency, responsibility, deliberation, or voluntary action.

**The key distinction:** Exercising a received capacity is not the same as originating the authority by which that capacity is judged.

A Witness may spend, exchange, or circulate what it has received; it does not thereby become the issuing Authority.

## 4. Genesis 2–3: the command precedes the human determination

### 4.1 Genesis 2:16–17 KJV — the Word is prior

> And the LORD God commanded the man, saying, Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat:
>
> But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.

The command is given before the temptation narrated in Genesis 3. The boundary is not authored by Adam's preference, Eve's evaluation, or a collective agreement. It is given by the Lord.

**Textual observation:** The command and its boundary precede the human response.

The investigation therefore asks whether the human will is being exercised within a reference already given, or whether the human evaluation is being treated as a new authority capable of replacing that reference.

### 4.2 Genesis 3:1–7 KJV — competing testimony and human evaluation

The serpent questions the Word, contradicts its stated consequence, and presents an alternative account. Genesis 3:6 records the woman's evaluation: the tree was good for food, pleasant to the eyes, and desired to make one wise. She takes and eats; Adam also eats.

The text records real human perception, desire, evaluation, and action. It does not identify those human acts as the originating authority that established the prior command.

**Investigative distinction:** The capacity to evaluate is not identical to the authority to originate the standard of good and evil.

The currency analogy asks whether the human Witness attempted to treat its own evaluation as self-issued authority. This is an interpretive question grounded in the contrast between the prior command and the competing testimony. It must not be expanded into the claim that all human desire, thought, choice, or evaluation is inherently wrong.

### 4.3 Genesis 3:22–24 — consequence and guarded access

The Lord's words in Genesis 3:22–24 follow the act and its consequences. The passage records God's action, the expulsion from the garden, and the guarding of the way to the tree of life.

The Witness must not turn the passage into an invented technical description of a monetary transaction. Its direct testimony concerns the human departure, God's response, and guarded access.

## 5. Scripture's direct language concerning God's will and human willing

The currency analogy must be tested against passages that explicitly speak of will, willing, purpose, and action. The words must be read in their own grammatical and canonical contexts.

### 5.1 John 1:12–13 KJV — not of the will of man

John writes that those who receive Christ and believe on His name are given power to become the sons of God. He then describes their birth:

> Which were born, not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God.

The text explicitly contrasts human willing with birth of God.

**Finding:** In the matter John is describing—becoming children of God—the originating cause is not human will. The passage does not deny that people believe or receive Christ; verse 12 has already named those responses. Verse 13 identifies the birth's source as God.

This is a particularly important text for the currency question: a human response is present, but the source of the birth is not credited to autonomous human issuance.

### 5.2 John 6:35–40, 44–45, 63–65 — the Father's will, giving, and granting

Jesus says that He came down from heaven not to do His own will, but the will of the Father who sent Him (John 6:38). He identifies the Father's will as that He lose nothing of all that the Father has given Him, but raise it up at the last day (6:39), and that everyone who sees the Son and believes on Him may have everlasting life (6:40).

Later Jesus says no one can come to Him unless the Father draws him (6:44), and that those who have heard and learned of the Father come to Him (6:45). In 6:63 Jesus says the Spirit gives life. In 6:65 He says no one can come unless it is given by the Father.

**Finding:** The passage places the Father's will, giving, drawing, teaching, and granting before and around the real human responses of coming and believing. The human response is not erased; it is not presented as the independent origin of the Father's purpose.

### 5.3 Romans 9:14–18 — not of him that willeth

Paul writes:

> So then it is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that sheweth mercy. (Romans 9:16, KJV)

The immediate argument concerns God's purpose, mercy, and freedom in showing mercy. The verse explicitly refuses to locate the decisive source of mercy in the human will or effort.

The surrounding context must be retained: Paul's argument begins with Israel, the promise, the children of promise, God's purpose according to election, and the examples of Isaac, Jacob, Esau, Moses, and Pharaoh. Romans 9 must not be isolated from Romans 10 and 11.

**Finding:** Human willing and effort are not the originating cause of God's mercy. The verse does not state that human willing is unreal; it denies that human willing is the source on which God's mercy depends.

### 5.4 Philippians 2:12–13 — willing and doing

Paul tells the believers to work out their own salvation with fear and trembling, then gives the reason:

> For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure. (Philippians 2:13, KJV)

This passage is central to the proposed analogy.

The text names both:
- **to will**;
- **to do**.

And it identifies God as the One working in the believers in both.

**Finding:** The passage does not remove human willing or action. It locates God's work as the source operating in the willing and doing. The human response is real, but it is not presented as self-originating.

The verse does not provide a complete mechanical account of how divine operation and human willing relate at every moment. It does establish that the willing and doing of which Paul speaks cannot be detached from God's work.

### 5.5 James 4:13–16 — human plans under “if the Lord will”

James addresses people who announce plans to travel, trade, and gain profit. He reminds them that they do not know what tomorrow will bring and that life is a vapour. He directs them to say:

> For that ye ought to say, If the Lord will, we shall live, and do this, or that. (James 4:15, KJV)

The issue is not that making plans is forbidden. The correction is against boasting in self-assured plans while ignoring dependence upon the Lord.

**Finding:** Human planning and action are real, but the person does not possess independent authority over life, time, or the outcome. The Lord's will remains the reference.

### 5.6 1 Corinthians 4:7 — what was not received?

Paul asks:

> For who maketh thee to differ from another? and what hast thou that thou didst not receive? now if thou didst receive it, why dost thou glory, as if thou hadst not received it?

The immediate subject is pride and boasting in the Corinthian community. Paul's questions expose the error of treating what was received as though it originated independently in the possessor.

**Finding:** This passage supplies a strong general safeguard for the currency analogy: possession does not establish origination. What is received cannot rightly be claimed as self-originated.

## 7. What the currency analogy reveals about modern theological use of “Free Will”

The phrase *free will* cannot be judged by its label alone. Its meaning must be established from the actual claims of the theologian, confession, or tradition being examined.

For this investigation, the following distinctions are required:

**Voluntary action:** A person acts willingly rather than by external compulsion. This does not by itself establish independent origination.

**Creaturely agency:** A person genuinely perceives, desires, deliberates, chooses, and acts within Creation. This does not make the person the Author.

**Moral responsibility:** Scripture addresses people concerning what they do. This must be preserved alongside the passages identifying God's initiative and sovereignty.

**Independent authority:** Human will is treated as the final originating standard, able to establish its own authority apart from the Author's Word. This is the specific claim that conflicts with the governing source relationship.

A fair theological investigation must quote each position accurately and identify which claim it actually makes. Some traditions use *free will* to defend voluntary action or responsibility while denying that human choice is independent of God. Others make stronger claims about the decisive priority or independence of human choice. Those positions cannot be treated as identical without examining their own primary texts.

**The GUA-W question is therefore not simply, “Do people have a will?” It is, “Who is the originating Authority, and can a derivative will issue itself as an independent authority?”**

## 10. Scientific and economic comparisons: bounded use of the analogy

The currency analogy can be examined through economics and systems concepts, but these comparisons must not be mistaken for proof of theology.

### 10.1 Issuance versus circulation

Money may circulate widely without every claim about its origin being true. Circulation describes movement and use; issuance concerns the authority and process by which a currency is brought into the system.

**Comparison:** Collective use or agreement about a claim does not establish its originating authority.

**Classification:** Comparable relationship.

### 10.2 Counterfeit and authentication

A counterfeit instrument may imitate the appearance of an authorized one. Authentication therefore requires more than popularity, familiarity, or successful circulation; it requires checking the relevant authority and properties.

**Comparison:** A Witness must distinguish a claim's appearance, acceptance, and repetition from its source and authority.

**Classification:** Analogy only. Theological truth is not authenticated by a banknote test.

### 10.3 Unit of account and governing standard

A monetary unit provides a standard for expressing value within a defined system. If a collective changes its own standard, its internal calculations may remain consistent while no longer answering to the original standard.

**Comparison:** Internal consistency is not identical to Alignment with the originating reference.

**Classification:** Comparable relationship, not identical mechanism.

### 10.4 AI-generated consensus

Multiple AI systems may reproduce a common answer because they share training sources, data, retrieval material, evaluation incentives, or assumptions. Their agreement can be useful, but it does not create authority or independent provenance.

**Comparison:** AI can circulate and amplify received claims. It cannot become the originating Authority by generating, repeating, or coordinating them.

**Classification:** Comparable epistemic relationship.

No economic model, financial instrument, or scientific measurement establishes God's sovereignty. These disciplines illuminate limited relationships only.
