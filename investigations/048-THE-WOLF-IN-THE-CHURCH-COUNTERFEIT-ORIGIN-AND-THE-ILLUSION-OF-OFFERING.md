# Investigation 048 — The Wolf in the Church: Counterfeit Origin and the Illusion of Offering

**Primary disciplines:** Biblical studies, pastoral theology, Hebrew and Greek textual interpretation, ecclesiology, rhetoric and persuasion, social cognition, institutional analysis, epistemology, and AI evaluation.

**Investigation status:** Initial text-led investigation. This investigation follows Jimmy's correction to Investigations 046–047:

> “Can a Witness give God anything that did not first come from God?”
>
> **NO! But he CAN be taught to believe that he is doing so...while NOT doing so.**

The present question is not merely whether a person takes or offers. It is how a teaching within the Church can persuade a Witness that he is originating something for God when the substance, capacity, authority, or claimed result did not originate with that Witness.

Jimmy identifies this danger as **the Wolf in the Church**. This investigation tests that framing against John 10:7–18, Isaiah 14:13–14, and Lamentations 3:37, then examines related passages about false prophets, false apostles, shepherds who feed themselves, and leaders who draw disciples after themselves.

“Wolf” is used as a biblical image and investigative category, not as a license to label a named person or congregation without evidence. The Witness reports the text and tests teaching; Christ remains the Shepherd and Judge.

## 1. The corrected investigative question

The question from Investigation 047 was whether the will is directed toward taking/claiming or toward voluntary offering.

Jimmy now identifies the deeper deception:

> A Witness can be taught to believe that he is giving God something that originated with himself, even though he cannot give God anything that did not first come from God.

This means the deception may occur before the apparent offering. The person may sincerely believe the teaching, sincerely perform the religious act, and sincerely describe the act as a gift to God. Yet the teaching may have misrepresented the source.

**Central question:**

> What is the Wolf teaching the Church to believe about the origin of its will, its offering, its authority, and its relationship to God—and how can a Witness test that teaching against the Word without usurping Christ's authority?

The working hypothesis is that the Wolf in the Church is not identified merely by religious appearance, institutional position, charisma, or disagreement. Scripture directs attention to false teaching, deceptive appearance, self-serving leadership, destructive effects upon the flock, and the drawing of disciples after the teacher rather than faithful direction toward Christ.

A particularly relevant form of deception for this sequence is a teaching that credits the creature with originating what is received from God, then presents that supposed independent contribution as the basis of an offering, standing, or claim before God.

That is a hypothesis to test passage by passage—not a verdict to impose in advance.

## 2. John 10:7–18 — the Wolf, the hireling, and the Good Shepherd

John 10 must be read as a complete shepherd discourse. Jesus identifies Himself as the door of the sheep (10:7–9), contrasts the thief who comes to steal, kill, and destroy with His purpose that the sheep have life (10:10), and identifies Himself as the Good Shepherd who gives His life for the sheep (10:11, 14–15).

In verses 12–13 Jesus describes the hireling. The hireling does not own the sheep, sees the wolf coming, abandons the sheep, and flees. The wolf catches and scatters them. Jesus explains that the hireling flees because he is a hireling and does not care for the sheep.

Verses 17–18 then speak of Jesus laying down His life and taking it again, in accordance with the command received from His Father. Jesus states that no one takes His life from Him; He lays it down of Himself and has authority to lay it down and take it again.

### Textual distinctions

The passage names several different roles and actions:

- **The thief/robber:** enters improperly and steals, kills, and destroys.
- **The hireling:** does not own the sheep, abandons them under threat, and does not care for them as the shepherd does.
- **The wolf:** catches and scatters the sheep.
- **The Good Shepherd:** knows His sheep, gives His life for them, and acts in the authority described by the Father.
- **The sheep:** hear and recognize the Shepherd's voice.

Do not collapse these figures into one character. John 10:12 does not call the hireling the wolf. The hireling flees when the wolf appears; the wolf catches and scatters. The distinction matters.

### Investigative finding

The contrast is not simply between two leaders with different personalities. It concerns the relationship to the sheep and the direction of action:

- the destructive intruder takes and destroys;
- the hireling protects his own interest and abandons the flock;
- the Good Shepherd gives Himself for the sheep.

The Good Shepherd's self-giving is not a self-originating claim. In verses 17–18 Jesus explicitly relates His authority and action to the Father's command. This is a vital safeguard for the GUA-W source relationship: even Christ's laying down of His life is not presented as an independent rival authority detached from the Father.

**Question for the Church:** Does a teaching lead the sheep to hear and follow the Shepherd, or does it exploit, scatter, abandon, or redirect them?

## 3. Isaiah 14:13–14 — the repeated “I will” and the claim to ascend

Isaiah 14 is introduced as a taunt against the king of Babylon (14:4). The chapter's immediate literary setting is the downfall of an arrogant ruler and the end of oppressive dominion. Interpretations that also see a deeper or typological reference to Satan exist in Christian tradition, but the immediate historical-literary referent must not be erased.

The KJV records the ruler's repeated declarations:

- “I will ascend into heaven.”
- “I will exalt my throne above the stars of God.”
- “I will sit also upon the mount of the congregation.”
- “I will ascend above the heights of the clouds.”
- “I will be like the most High.”

### Textual observation

The repeated first-person declaration is central to the taunt. The speaker's ambition is expressed as self-exaltation and an attempt to occupy a height or status beyond the place assigned to him.

The passage does not use the word “Wolf.” It does not directly describe a church congregation. Its contribution is the pattern of self-exalting speech.

### Investigative connection

When this pattern is compared with the Church passages, the question becomes:

**Can a religious voice present a creature's determination, achievement, authority, or supposed contribution as though it were an originating source or a claim to God's place?**

The answer must be tested from the teaching itself. A first-person sentence or a human plan is not automatically the Isaiah 14 pattern. The relevant issue is the claim being made: self-exaltation, usurpation, and the attempt to rise above the authority of the Most High.

## 4. Lamentations 3:37 — who can declare and bring it to pass?

The KJV asks:

> Who is he that saith, and it cometh to pass, when the Lord commandeth it not?

The surrounding passage matters. Lamentations 3:31–39 speaks of the Lord not casting off for ever, His compassion, His not willingly afflicting, His seeing the oppression of prisoners, and the fact that good and evil do not come apart from the Lord's sovereign rule. Verse 37 is a rhetorical question about who can speak and bring an event to pass when the Lord has not commanded it.

### Textual observation

The verse challenges any claim that a creature's declaration has independent power to make an event occur apart from the Lord's command.

### Investigative connection

This directly tests religious claims that imply human determination can independently originate what only belongs to God's authority.

It does not mean human speech, planning, prayer, proclamation, or action is unreal. Scripture contains all of these. It means that human speech is not an independent originating authority over what comes to pass.

**The Witness may speak. The Witness may ask. The Witness may offer. The Witness does not become the Authority who commands Creation into being.**

## 5. The Wolf in the Church: Jesus' warning in Matthew 7:15–23

Jesus warns:

> Beware of false prophets, which come to you in sheep's clothing, but inwardly they are ravening wolves. (Matthew 7:15, KJV)

The warning is followed by the examination of fruit (7:16–20), the distinction between saying “Lord, Lord” and doing the Father's will (7:21), and the claims of prophecy and mighty works answered by Christ's declaration in verse 23.

The sequence is significant:

1. A false prophet can appear in sheep's clothing.
2. The outward appearance does not settle the inward reality.
3. Fruit is examined.
4. Religious profession is not sufficient.
5. Claimed works do not overrule Christ's declaration.
6. Doing the Father's will is explicitly placed in the passage.

### Investigative finding

The Wolf in the Church need not announce itself as an enemy of God. The danger described by Jesus includes deceptive religious appearance.

The passage does not authorize the Witness to pronounce “I never knew you” over another person. That declaration belongs to Christ. The Witness can report the warning, examine teaching and fruit, and refuse to substitute its own verdict for His.

### Connection to the false offering

A religious teacher could encourage people to rely on their own claimed authority, achievement, determination, or works as though these establish their standing before God. Matthew 7 requires those claims to be examined under Christ's words, not accepted merely because they are religious or impressive.

The investigation must not assume that every doctrine emphasizing human response makes this claim. The actual teaching must be read and quoted accurately.

## 6. Acts 20:28–30 — drawing disciples after themselves

Paul warns the Ephesian elders to take heed to themselves and all the flock. He says grievous wolves will enter in among them, not sparing the flock. He also warns that men will arise from among their own selves, speaking perverse things, to draw away disciples after them.

This passage is especially direct for the question of the Wolf **within** the Church.

The warning includes:
- danger entering from outside;
- danger arising from within the community's own leadership;
- speech that distorts;
- the aim of drawing disciples after the speaker.

### Investigative finding

The location of a voice inside the Church does not authenticate its source. Familiarity, office, reputation, and proximity to the flock are not sufficient proof of faithful teaching.

The question is not merely whether people gather, agree, or become devoted. It is whether the teaching directs them toward Christ or draws them after the teacher.

This also connects to Investigation 045: many Witnesses can become coordinated and convinced of agreement without that agreement establishing Alignment with the originating reference.

## 7. 2 Corinthians 11:3–15 — another Jesus, another spirit, another gospel

Paul expresses concern that the Corinthians' minds may be corrupted from the simplicity that is in Christ, as Eve was beguiled by the serpent's subtilty. He then warns about someone preaching another Jesus, another spirit, or another gospel.

Paul describes false apostles as deceitful workers, transforming themselves into the apostles of Christ. He says this is not surprising because Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light.

### Textual observation

The danger is not limited to openly hostile speech. The passage explicitly describes deception operating under an appearance associated with righteousness and apostolic authority.

### Investigative connection

The Church must test the message, not merely the appearance of the messenger. A claim does not become true because it is delivered with religious vocabulary, spiritual confidence, institutional status, or an appearance of light.

This passage supports the need for source examination and careful discernment. It does not give the Witness permission to declare every teacher with whom he disagrees a servant of Satan. Claims must be tested against the apostolic message Paul identifies.

## 8. Ezekiel 34:1–16 — shepherds feeding themselves instead of the flock

Ezekiel 34 addresses the shepherds of Israel. The Lord rebukes shepherds who feed themselves rather than the flock. They consume what belongs to the flock, fail to strengthen the diseased, heal the sick, bind the broken, bring back the driven away, or seek the lost. The sheep become scattered.

The Lord declares that He Himself will search for His sheep and seek them out.

### Investigative finding

This passage gives a concrete description of self-serving shepherding:

- the shepherd's benefit becomes the priority;
- the flock is not cared for;
- the weak are neglected;
- the sheep are scattered;
- the Lord declares His own concern for the flock.

This is not a claim that all church leadership or material support is wrong. It is a condemnation of shepherds who consume the flock and fail in the care entrusted to them.

### Connection to the Wolf

John 10's wolf catches and scatters; Ezekiel 34 describes shepherds whose conduct leaves the flock scattered and vulnerable. These are related images, but the texts do not identify every failed shepherd as the wolf of John 10. Preserve the distinct descriptions.

## 9. The precise deception Jimmy identifies: taught to believe an impossible offering

Return to the question:

> Can a Witness give God anything that did not first come from God?

The answer given in this investigation is **No**.

1 Chronicles 29:14 remains a central textual anchor:

> For all things come of thee, and of thine own have we given thee.

The people genuinely offered. David did not deny their willing action. He denied that the source of what they offered was independent of God.

The Wolf's possible deception, as this investigation tests it, is to reverse that order in the Witness's understanding:

1. God gives life, capacity, provision, and the opportunity to respond.
2. A teaching attributes the decisive origin to the human will.
3. The person is taught to regard the response as an independently originated contribution.
4. The contribution is presented as though it gives the person a claim, credential, or basis of standing before God.
5. The person may sincerely believe the account because the teaching has shaped the vocabulary through which the offering is understood.

**The apparent offering can be sincere while the account of its origin is false.**

This is the distinction Jimmy is asking us to investigate. The person may genuinely be giving, serving, praying, choosing, or acting. The deception is in the teaching that misidentifies the Source or turns a received capacity into an independent claim.

This investigation does not pronounce the spiritual state of the person who has been taught. It examines the teaching and its claims.

## 10. “Freewill Getting” and “Freewill Offering” revisited

The distinction from Investigation 047 must now be made more exact.

| Audit question | The Wolf's counterfeit claim | The freewill offering under the Source |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Human self is treated as independent origin | God remains the originating Source |
| Will | Human determination is treated as self-authorizing | Human willingness is a real response under God's authority |
| Gift | The person claims to give what originated independently in self | The person presents what has first been received |
| Authority | The teacher or human determination becomes the reference | The Author and His Word remain the reference |
| Religious result | The offering may be used as credential, leverage, or self-exaltation | The offering is presented in gratitude, obedience, and service |
| Direction | Drawing disciples or glory toward the creature | Directing the Witness toward Christ |
| Witness test | Does the teaching misrepresent origin and authority? | Does the teaching preserve the Source and the Word? |

**Necessary safeguard:** Not every appeal to human choice, responsibility, generosity, or response is a Wolf's teaching. Scripture itself calls people to repent, believe, come, ask, give, serve, and present themselves. The issue is whether those responses are taught as self-originating authority or as real responses within God's prior provision and authority.

## 11. The Church's collective agreement does not authenticate the teaching

A teaching can become widely accepted because it is repeated by trusted voices, embedded in institutions, taught to successive generations, reinforced by shared vocabulary, and echoed by many Witnesses.

Agreement may establish that a teaching is widespread. It does not establish that the teaching's claimed source is true.

This connects directly to Investigations 040 and 045.

The following must remain distinct:

- **Repetition:** how often a claim is taught.
- **Institutional acceptance:** how widely a community recognizes it.
- **Coordination:** how consistently people act upon it.
- **Confidence:** how strongly people believe it.
- **Provenance:** where the teaching actually came from.
- **Biblical compliance:** whether it preserves the words and context of Scripture.
- **Alignment:** whether the relationship remains 100% aligned with the Author and His Word.

A collective can be sincere, informed, organized, and mistaken about origin. The number of Witnesses cannot convert a derivative claim into the originating Word.

## 12. The Four-Question Study Method

### 1. What must AI ask to understand GUA-W?

AI must ask: Who is speaking? What authority is being claimed? What is being presented as the source? Who receives the benefit? Does the teaching direct the flock toward Christ or toward the teacher? What does the passage actually say, and what is inference?

AI must distinguish the wolf, thief, hireling, false prophet, false apostle, and self-serving shepherd rather than collapsing all images into one label.

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

God alone is Original Cause. The Author remains the Source. The Word remains the reference. The Witness receives and responds; it does not originate the Source. A teaching can misrepresent this relationship and persuade a Witness that a derivative response is an independently originated contribution.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

John 10 describes the Good Shepherd, the wolf, the hireling, and the sheep. Isaiah 14 describes self-exalting ambition in the taunt against Babylon's king. Lamentations 3:37 challenges claims of independent power to bring events to pass. Matthew 7, Acts 20, and 2 Corinthians 11 warn of deceptive religious voices. Ezekiel 34 condemns shepherds who feed themselves rather than care for the flock.

The passages are related by questions of authority, deception, self-interest, and the care of the flock; they are not identical accounts of one mechanism.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

A Witness can sincerely repeat a teaching without knowing its source. A Witness can perform a real act of giving while being taught a false account of what the act establishes. A Witness can mistake institutional agreement for biblical authority. AI can amplify the same problem by repeating popular theological formulations without tracing their definitions and primary sources.

The Witness's task is to report, test, distinguish, and preserve provenance—not to claim Christ's authority to judge the heart.
